\begin{abstract}

A tight connection between generative control policies and sampling-based predictive control (SBPC) has recently gained attention in robotics. Enabled by modern computing architectures, sampling-based predictive control can generate multi-modal action sequences under model-based safety constraints, whereas conditional flow matching amortizes these behaviors into a fast generative policy. However, a pretrained generative policy can assign little probability to safe action windows in out-of-distribution (OOD) contexts. To address these issues, we propose the offline Safe Flow Expansion framework built upon model predictive path integral control (MPPI) and conditional flow matching (CFM). With the safety-conditioned receding-horizon control distribution learned from MPPI demonstrations, our framework self-generates and uses deterministic verification to collect multi-step verifier-positive windows. We evaluate the performance of our adaptive design in safety-critical settings in both 2D and 3D unseen environments.

% Sampling-based predictive control (SBPC) algorithms, such as model predictive path integral (MPPI), have become a popular method for robotic motion planning applications due to their computational efficiency on modern computing architectures. However, it is difficult to enforce hard safety constraints for such techniques because they average over samples to realize the final set of control inputs. While individual samples may satisfy a given constraint, the sample average can violate the safety requirement. Similarly, diffusion and flow matching algorithms have become a popular way to generate robot motion trajectories. In this paper, we introduce a novel strategy to learn a conditional generative model that allows for online tuning of a safety parameter. As for sampling-based controllers, it is difficult to enforce hard safety-critical constraints in these methods. To address these limitations, this paper proposes a safety-critical framework for SBPCs and generative trajectory generation, based on the framework of discrete-time control barrier functions (DCBFs). Our DCBF-based method ensures safety during both the sampling and averaging phases of SBPCs, efficiently producing a rich dataset of multi-modal, collision-free behaviors. We then train a conditional flow matching model on these data to learn a generative policy that can be modulated at inference time by a single safety parameter. We demonstrate our approach for cluttered environments for a double integrator and a quadrotor simulation. Our results indicate that the proposed MPPI-DCBF framework consistently achieves superior safety guarantees compared to baseline MPPI methods, together with a context-aware conditional flow matching model, paving the way for real-time, safe SBPC and generative robotic control.

\section{Introduction}

Sampling-based predictive control (SBPC) has been gaining traction in the nonlinear optimal control community. Completely general optimal control approaches often suffer from computational complexity associated with solving stochastic Hamilton--Jacobi--Bellman equations backwards in time, commonly referred to as the curse of dimensionality. SBPC is an alternative to gradient-based model predictive control (MPC), where a simple sampling procedure is used in place of a sophisticated nonlinear optimizer \cite{posa2014direct, kurtz2023inverse, aydinoglu2024consensus}. Algorithms in this family include model predictive path integral control (MPPI) \cite{williams2016aggressive}, predictive sampling \cite{howell2022predictive}, and cross-entropy methods \cite{rubinstein1999cross}. MPPI, for example, circumvents computational complexity by employing forward sampling of stochastic dynamics combined with Monte Carlo approximation techniques \cite{williams2017model}. 
% The optimal control is then computed as the exponentially weighted average of the sampled trajectories, enabling real-time implementation.
SBPC algorithms are particularly well-suited to recent advances in parallel computing architectures and parallel simulation \cite{mjx, Genesis, makoviychuk2021isaac}, enabling them to scale to complex problems like in-hand dexterous manipulation \cite{li2025drop}, legged locomotion \cite{xue2025full}, and more \cite{howell2022predictive}.

Despite these advantages, SBPCs such as MPPI inherently struggle with safety guarantees, such as collision avoidance.
Safety constraints are typically incorporated indirectly through penalty terms in the cost function~\cite{williams2017model, mohamed2023gp}.
% that are inversely proportional to the robot's proximity to obstacles and heavily penalize the collision 
However, relying solely on these penalties lacks explicit safety guarantees due to the nature of the sampling averaging procedure. 
This limitation motivates our goal to first integrate safety-critical constraints explicitly into the SBPC frameworks.
% \note{Need a brief sentence and an reference about safety filters at the beginning of the next paragraph.}
A common method to ensure safety in collision avoidance tasks is to employ a safety filter, which minimally modifies the output of a potentially unsafe nominal controller to guarantee the satisfaction of the constraints \cite{ames2014cbf}. However, a simple safety filter applied \textit{only} to the control output is insufficient to guarantee safety due to the tight coupling between control objectives and long-term safety criteria that are potentially conflicting. Previous works have used control Lyapunov functions (CLF) and control barrier functions (CBF), often with relaxation factors, to solve this issue as an optimization problem, for mediating safety and feasibility \cite{ames2016control, zeng2021enhancing}.
% or solve the optimization problem in the context of model predictive control (MPC) to take future information into account over a longer horizon \cite{zeng2021safety}. 
Recent works have encoded this concept through predictive safety filters, which leverage predictive CBFs in layered control architectures \cite{compton2024learning}. However, in cluttered environments, this direct optimization often becomes non-convex and intractable.

This computational burden motivates a shift from online constrained optimization to learning-based control, where complex planning is performed offline to curate datasets of safe, multi-modal behavior. Generative models, such as diffusion and flow matching, can synthesize motion trajectories from diverse, high-dimensional demonstrations \cite{kurtz2025generative, chi2023diffusion}. However, they do not natively provide formal safety guarantees during rollout. Previous work attempts to encode safety by conditioning or regularizing generation \cite{dai2025safefm, carvalho2023mpdiffusion}, but deployments still lack mechanisms to systematically trade off safety and feasibility. This can be critical in real-world tasks where the robot must adjust caution to context. 
Recent works have incorporated MPC into refinement of generation to guarantee local optimality in its multi-modal behavior that is induced by reward-based guidance \cite{mizuta2025unified}. This allows generative methods to account for both safety and feasibility, but it can struggle to generalize to out-of-distribution (OOD) environments, because this reward-tilting can overly optimize the learned distribution \cite{dandapanthula2026we}.

A fundamental challenge in generative motion planning and control is addressing this distribution shift while maintaining safety and feasibility. Recent works suggest that self-generation using uncertainty-aware sampling in FM can be used to expand the generable set with probabilistic guarantee through an iterative verification and update process \cite{de2026active}. We extend this concept to a conditional flow matching (CFM) model, explicitly capturing the intrinsic trade-off between safety and feasibility through a verification process to generate a safe policy. To this end, we introduce the \textit{Safe Flow Expansion} framework, a novel approach for \textit{distilling safety} from a robust predictive planner into a generative policy.

Our generative policy builds upon generative predictive control (GPC), which learns a vector field to map noise to control actions conditioned on observations and then applies control in a receding horizon fashion~\cite{kurtz2025generative}. \section{Preliminaries}
\subsection{Model Predictive Path Integral control}
In discrete time, MPPI solves the following non-convex optimization problem at time step $k$ and state $\mathbf{x}(0) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x}$ over the mean control sequence $\{ \mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_u}$ for $i \in  \{0, ..., N-1\}$
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:mppi}
\begin{align}
\min_{\{\mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k}\}}
& \mathbb{E}\!\left[
\phi\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{k+N\mid k}\right)
\!+\!\!\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\Big(q(\mathbf{x}_{k+i\mid k})\!+\!\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k}^\top R\,\mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k}\Big)
\right], \label{eq:mppi-cost} \tag{1a}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
& \mathbf{x}_{k+i+1\mid k}=f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{k+i\mid k},\,\mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k}+\varepsilon_{k+i}\right), 
\label{eq:mppi-dyn}  \tag{1b}\\
& \mathbf{x}_{k+i\mid k} \in \mathcal{X}, \quad \mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k} \in \mathcal{U}, \label{eq:mppi-set1} \tag{1c} \\
& \mathbf{x}_{k\mid k}=\mathbf{x}_k, \quad \mathbf{x}_{k+H-1\mid k} \in \mathcal{X}_f,\qquad \label{eq:mppi-set2} \tag{1d}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Here, $N$ is the $q: \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is any running cost, $\phi : \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is a terminal cost, $\varepsilon_{k+i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\,\Sigma)$ is the control disturbance, $\Sigma$ is the control disturbance variance, $R \in \mathbb{R}^{n_u \times n_u}$ is the control cost weight.
% For the collision avoidance task, the controller can reason about crashing by setting collision penalties using the signed clearance to the closest obstacle $d_{\min}(\mathbf{x})$ in their running cost function, such as
% \begin{equation}
% \begin{aligned}
% q(\mathbf{x})
% &=(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{goal}})^\top Q (\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{goal}})\\
% &\quad+\kappa_{\text{soft}}\,e^{-\alpha\,d_{\min}(\mathbf{x})}
% +\kappa_{\text{hard}}\,\mathbf{1}\!\bigl[d_{\min}(\mathbf{x})<0\bigr].
% \label{eq:mppisoftconstraint}
% \end{aligned}
% \end{equation}
The MPPI optimal control sequence is a path-integral approximation of reward-weighted average random variations, which is solved by Monte Carlo methods, centered on the optimal policy sequence at the previous time step \cite{williams2017model}. The unexecuted part of the sequence is shifted by one time step and acts as the warm start of the next optimization. 

Let this warm start sequence from the previous time step be denoted by $\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{k+i\mid k}$. For rollout $m=1,\dots,M$ with perturbations $\varepsilon^{(m)}_{k+i}\!\sim\!\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)$, MPPI measures cost per rollout via forward sampling of random controls and states that can be expressed as
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
\mathbf{u}^{(m)}_{k+i\mid k}&=\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{k+i\mid k}+\varepsilon^{(m)}_{k+i},\label{eq:mppi_rollout}\\
\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+i+1\mid k}&=f\!\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+i\mid k},\,\mathbf{u}^{(m)}_{k+i\mid k}\bigr).
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Then, the cost for each rollout is
\begin{equation}
S^{(m)}\!=\!\phi\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+N\mid k}\bigr)
+\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\Big(q\,\!\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+i\mid k}\bigr)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{u}^{(m)\top}_{k+i\mid k} R\,\mathbf{u}^{(m)}_{k+i\mid k}\Big).
\label{eq:mppicost}
\end{equation}
Information-theoretic MPPI uses soft-min importance weights \cite{williams2017info}:
\begin{equation}
\omega_m=\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{\lambda_M}\,[S^{(m)}-S_\text{min}]\Bigr),\qquad
\beta=\min_m S^{(m)}, \label{eq:mppi-weights}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_M >0$ is a temperature which controls how heavily the optimal trajectory is weighted, and $S_\text{min}$ is the minimum rollout cost, used to improve the numerical stability. Then, for $i \in \{0, ..., N-1\}$, the mean update that gives optimal control sequence is
\begin{equation}
\Delta \mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k}=
\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\omega_m\,\varepsilon^{(m)}_{k+i}}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\omega_m},
\quad
\mathbf{u}^*_{k+i\mid k}\leftarrow \bar{\mathbf{u}}_{k+i\mid k}+\Delta \mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k}. \label{eq:mppi-update}
\end{equation}
Then the current optimal control $\mathbf{u}^*_{k\mid k}$ is applied. 
% & h^{\mathrm{aff}}\!\left(x_{k+i\mid k};\,x_{k\mid k}\right)\ \ge\ (1-\gamma)^i,\quad i=0,\dots,N. \tag{1d}\label{eq:safemppi}
This parallelizable sampling process makes MPPI highly suitable for solving non-convex optimal control problems on modern GPU hardware. However, a key challenge arises in safety-critical applications. When obstacles are present, a cost for avoiding obstacles does not provide formal safety guarantees, as the final control is averaged over many trajectories. 

Several works have focused on incorporating safety into the forward sampling process. Approaches that shape the rollout distribution or impose barrier constraints \cite{yin2022,yin2023shield} bias the sampling toward safety, but often lack a rigorous formal definition. Conversely, methods that act as sampling-based safety filters by greedily selecting safe samples \cite{rabiee2024guaranteed} enforce safety but bypass the averaging process in \eqref{eq:mppi-update}, thereby sacrificing the optimality certificate.

\subsection{Conditional Flow Matching for trajectory generation}
Conditional Flow matching is increasingly used in robotics for trajectory generation and policy generation \cite{kurtz2025generative,mizuta2025unified}. The trajectory generation process learns a mapping from noise to entire collision-free trajectories by end-to-end supervised learning, but it can struggle to generalize to out-of-distribution environments and may require complete retraining \cite{ye2024efficient, dai2025safefm}. 
% In contrast, (conditional) flow matching-based policy generation is more flexible, focuses on learning a mapping to a lower-dimensional control action, conditioned on observations \cite{kurtz2025generative}.

Here, we describe the important concept of conditional flow matching. For collision avoidance, it is essential to generate a trajectory conditioned on geometric observations while benefiting from offline flow matching training and fast computation from SBPC. To achieve this, the generative model predicts actions conditioned on the given observation $\mathbf{o_t}$ and executes for a short horizon \cite{chi2023diffusion}. 

Concretely, at the current discrete time step $k$, the model takes $O$ steps of observations $\mathbf{o}_{k:k+O-1}$, predicts $H$ steps of actions $\mathbf{\hat u}_{k:k+H-1}$, execute $A$ steps of predicted actions $\mathbf{\hat u}_{k:k+A-1}$. Here, $O$ is the observation horizon, $H$ is the planning horizon, $A$ is the execution horizon of the action. Throughout our experiments, we used fixed values of $O = 1, H = 10, A = 1$. 

It is important to note that our framework utilizes a safety-critical MPPI planner as a pre-trained model and \textit{distills its safety} to a conditional flow matching. Here, $\mathbf{v}_t(\cdot)$ denotes the flow matching vector field that is learned to regress the behavior of the planner. We use \(\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_u}\) for a target optimal control policy, \textit{i.e.,} the symbol \(\mathbf{u}^*_{k\mid k}\) used in the previous section.

Conditional Flow matching aims to learn a marginal vector field $\mathbf{v}^*_t(x)$ that converts a simple distribution $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$ into a complex data distribution $z \sim p_{data}$. This is done by considering the marginal probability path $p_t(x)$ that evolves from $\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$ to $p_{data}$. With an optimal transport probability path that drives noise $\epsilon$ to $\mathbf{z}$ with constant direction and speed in time~\cite{lipman2022flow}, the conditional flow matching (CFM) model $\mathbf{v}_t^\theta(x)$ learns the conditional vector field $\mathbf{v}^*_t(x|\mathbf{z})$ per sampled point $x$ from conditional vector field by minimizing the mean-squared loss. :
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}(\theta)&=
\mathbb{E}_{\substack{
t\sim \mathrm{Unif}[0,1],\\
\mathbf{z}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}},\\ \mathbf{x}\sim p_t(\cdot\mid z)
}} \notag
\!\Bigl[\bigl\|\mathbf{v}_t^\theta(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{v}_t^*(\mathbf{x}\mid \mathbf{z})\bigr\|^2\Bigr]
\label{eq:cfm}\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{\substack{
t\sim \mathrm{Unif}[0,1],\\ \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_d),\\
\mathbf{z}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}
}}
\!\Bigl[\bigl\|\mathbf{v}_t^\theta(t\mathbf{z}+(1-t)\epsilon)-(\mathbf{z}-\epsilon)\bigr\|^2\Bigr].
\end{align}

To generate a policy using the CFM model, we first assume there is a ground-truth marginal vector field that transforms noise into an optimal policy given observations. In our case, we view the task of generating safe robot motion trajectories conditioned on local geometric observations and states $\mathbf{o}$, a safety parameter $\gamma$. In this case, we need to expand the expectation from \eqref{eq:cfm} over all possible $o$, $\gamma$, and $c$. Thus, we define our training objective as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta)\!=\!
\mathbb{E}_{\substack{
t\sim \mathrm{Unif}[0,1],\\ \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_d),\\
(z,o,\gamma)\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}(z,o,\gamma)
}}
\!\!\!\!\Bigl[\bigl\|\mathbf{v}_t^\theta(tz+(1-t)\epsilon\!\mid\! o,\gamma)-(z-\epsilon)\bigr\|^2\Bigr].
\label{eq:cfm_cost}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Discrete-Time Control Barrier Functions}
Let $\mathcal{C}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^n:\; h(x)\ge 0\}$ denote the safe set for a continuously differentiable $h$.
For the discrete‐time control‐affine system
\begin{equation}
    x_{k+1}=f(x_k)+g(x_k)u_k, \label{eq:affinesystem}
\end{equation}
a function $h$ is a discrete‐time control barrier function (DCBF) associated with the control system \eqref{eq:affinesystem} if there exists $\gamma\in(0,1]$ such that for all $x_k$ there exists a control $u_k$ with
\begin{equation}
    \Delta h(x_k,u_k)\;:=\;h(x_{k+1})-h(x_k)\;\ge\;-\gamma\,h(x_k).
    \label{eq:dtcbf}
\end{equation}
Condition \eqref{eq:dtcbf} implies the one‐step decay bound
\begin{equation*}
    h(x_{k+1})\;\ge\;(1-\gamma)\,h(x_k)
    \, \Longrightarrow \,
    h(x_k)\;\ge\;(1-\gamma)^{k}\,h(x_0),
    \label{eq:exp_decay}
\end{equation*}
\textit{i.e.,} the lower bound on the barrier value decays exponentially with base $(1-\gamma)$, ensuring forward invariance of $\mathcal{C}$ \cite{ames2014cbf,agrawal2017discrete}.

\begin{remark} In order to prove $h$ is a valid DCBF, it is necessary to find a point-wise set of control values at any given state that is non-empty, with corresponding $\gamma$. To be compatible with the averaging in MPPI, we assumed that all rollouts \eqref{eq:mppi_rollout} assigned nonzero MPPI weight after rejection based on \eqref{eq:dtcbf}. In practice, we only used feasible sets of actions as data to train the generative policy.
\end{remark}

We define the safety specification that is derived from a supporting hyperplane to the single obstacle. Let \(\piq:\mathbb R^{n_x} \to \mathbb R^d\) be the projection that maps robot states into the robot position. \(\piq(\xki)=\qki\in\mathbb R^d\) denote the robot position at state \(\mathbf{x}_{k+i \mid k}\) at discrete time step $k$ and horizon $i = 0, ..., H-1$. For a convex obstacle, let $\mathbf{p}$ be the point on its boundary closest to the current position $\qk$, with outward normal $\mathbf{n}$. We define the barrier function as
\begin{equation}
h^{\mathrm{aff}}(\x;\xk) :=
\frac{\mathbf{n}^\top(\q-\p)}{\mathbf{n}^\top(\mathbf{q}_{k\mid k}-\mathbf{p})}.
\label{eq:aff_barrier}
\end{equation}
The implicit safe set for each horizon $i$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{aff}}_i=\{\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \mid h^{\mathrm{aff}}(\x;\xk)\ge (1-\gamma)^i\},\label{eq:aff_safeset}
\end{equation}
is a half-space, which is convex. Iterating the condition \eqref{eq:dtcbf} from $i=0$ with the normalization $h^{\mathrm{aff}}(\xk;\xk)=1$ yields an exponentially decaying lower bound on the safety specification along the prediction horizon:
\begin{equation}
h^{\mathrm{aff}}(\xki;\xk) \ge (1-\gamma)^i. \quad i = 0, ..., H-1.
\label{eq:safetyspecification}
\end{equation}
% Applying the condition \eqref{eq:dtcbf} to this affine barrier function provides our safety specification for each rollout:
% \begin{equation}
% h^{\mathrm{aff}}(x_{k+i+1\mid k};x_{k\mid k}) \ge (1-\gamma) h^{\mathrm{aff}}(x_{k+i\mid k};x_{k\mid k}).
% \end{equation}
\noindent \textbf{Nominal Polytope for MPPI safety.} We further define the notion of a \textit{nominal polytope} to generalize from above local safety definition to multiple obstacles. 
We assume that the robot can only sense obstacles within a finite radial distance $R$, and that the index of obstacles that intersect within that region at the current position is denoted by $\mathcal{O}(\xk)$. The barrier function is defined as the minimum of the affine barrier functions \eqref{eq:safetyspecification} over obstacles in the sensing region:
\begin{equation}
    h^{\text{poly}}(\x;\xk) = \min_{j\in\mathcal{O}_j} h^{\mathrm{aff}}_j(\x;\x_k)
\end{equation}
The implicit safe set for each horizon $i=0,\cdots,H-1$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}^{\text{poly}}_i=\{\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \mid h^{\text{poly}}(\x;\xk)\ge (1-\gamma)^i\},\label{eq:poly_safeset}
\end{equation}

Iterating this over nearby sensed obstacles yields convex polytope. The construction keeps the predicted discrete-time robot positions inside a conservative intersection of obstacle-separating half-spaces. Collision avoidance for a finite-size robot follows after configuration-space obstacle inflation, subject to the sensing, model, and intersample assumptions stated below.

\noindent \textbf{Remark}
A valid control barrier function on $\mathcal{U}=\mathbb{R}^{d_u}$ is usually assumed in prior works on SBPC safety, \footnote{If we assume $\mathcal{X}$ as a controlled invariant set, then the reachable set is also guaranteed to be non-empty.}. However, one must verify that there exists a \textit{feasible input-constrained controller} (\textit{i.e.,} input bounds in $\mathcal{U}\neq\mathbb{R}^{d_u}$), and promising approaches to address this are backup control barrier functions (bCBFs) \cite{gurriet2018online, van2026generalizations}.

\section{Problem Definition}

To achieve safety-critical policy generation, we first formulate our safe planning objective as a constrained optimization problem. Under pointwise feasibility and exact model, the executed next state satisfies the local nominal DTCBF condition:
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:sc-mppi}
\begin{align*}
\min_{\{\mathbf{u}_{k:k+H-1\mid k}\}}
& \mathbb{E}\!\left[J(\mathbf{x}_{k}, \mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k})
\right], \tag{1a} \label{eq:mppi-dcbf}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
& \eqref{eq:mppi-dyn}, \ \eqref{eq:mppi-set1}, \ \eqref{eq:mppi-set2},\\
& h^{\text{poly}}(\xki;\xk)\ge (1-\gamma)^i. \tag{1e} \label{eq:poly}
\end{align*}
\end{subequations}

Direct optimization using a non-convex control barrier function that replaces $h^\text{poly}$ reduces to the problem MPC-CBF in the previous work \cite{zeng2021safety}. Despite the recursive feasibility of both problems not being generally guaranteed, from the view of set invariance, the DTCBF decay,  $\gamma$, modulates the trade-off between \textit{safety and local feasibility}. For example, small $\gamma$ yields higher value of barrier function as can be directly seen from~\eqref{eq:poly}, but is less feasible.  
% (Here say something about their optimization problem's downside e.g., sensitive to initialization and struggle in dynamic settings...) 
However, the non-convex nature of the resulting optimization problem on arbitrary $J(\mathbf{x}_{k}, \mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k})$ often makes real-time implementation computationally intractable \cite{rawlings2020model}. SBPC methods such as MPPI face similar challenges, and often mitigate pointwise infeasibility by shaping the sampling distribution \cite{yin2022, yin2023shield}, but this trades-off safety and optimality. 

% However, in practice, constructing bCBFs in a task-agnostic way is challenging. 

For these reasons, our planner relies on the weaker but more practical condition of pointwise feasibility without harming the optimality condition. Rather than proving recursive feasibility, our approach ensures safety by enforcing DCBF constraints at each time step. This also motivates our next goal to develop a safe flow expansion mechanism, where the data is scarce in OOD. We rather self-generate the safe behavior and enforce more relaxed DCBF constraints. Rather than relying purely on online computation, we view the proposed safe flow expansion as a mechanism for \textit{distilling} this safe behavior into a generative policy. Having established the problem statement, we direct our attention to an example to answer the question: \textit{How can we train a generative model that also exhibits this safe behavior in out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios?}

% We consider a robot whose dynamics are described by the discrete-time control affine system in \eqref{eq:affinesystem}, evolving in a cluttered environment with static obstacles. This work has two primary objectives. First, we experimentally demonstrate that our proposed controller, MPPI-DCBF, achieves superior safety guarantees over a standard baseline MPPI with different choices of the safety parameter $\gamma$. We validate this approach and its scalability on both LTI systems and complex nonlinear models, such as a quadrotor. Second, we address the challenge of creating a real-time generative policy that can modulate the trade-off between safety and feasibility by continuously scheduling $\gamma$, based on observations.

\section{Method}

Our main idea is that with some scarce, conservative safe trajectories provided by the MPPI-DCBF planner, we can self-generate to new safe, less conservative actions that enforce the same recursive safety requirements. 

\subsection{The MPPI-DCBF Algorithm}

We first propose model predictive path integral control with DCBF-based sample rejection (MPPI-DCBF), which modifies the standard MPPI framework to formally enforce safety. The core of our method is the safety specification in \eqref{eq:poly}, which is designed to guarantee the forward invariance of the sequence of time-varying safe sets $\mathcal{C}_i^{\mathrm{poly}}$ defined in \eqref{eq:poly_safeset}. This is achieved by integrating the DCBF condition directly into the sampling step of the MPPI algorithm. Any sampled rollout that violates the condition is rejected; consequently, the optimal control sequence \eqref{eq:mppi-update} is computed as a convex combination of only those rollouts that are certified to be safe. Under the assumption of pointwise feasibility\footnote{For the discrete-time control affine system \eqref{eq:affinesystem} and for any initial state $x_{k|k} \in \mathcal{C}_i^\mathrm{poly}$, there exists at least one control sequence $\{u_{k+i|k}\}_{i=0}^{N-1}$ such that the resulting trajectory $\{x_{k+i|k}\}_{i=1}^{N}$ lies within the sequence of safe sets defined in \eqref{eq:poly_safeset}, i.e., $x_{k+i|k} \in \mathcal{C}_i^{\mathrm{poly}}$ for all $i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$.

\subsection{Multi-step Safety Verification}
Before we introduce our main algorithm, here we define the deterministic verifier function that ensures the trajectory respects multi-step safety. We named this polytope as \textit{verifier polytope} and separates the term with the nominal polytope \eqref{eq:poly}; it is constructed along the prediction horizon $H$ in the trajectory yielded by the generated action. 

\noindent \textbf{Verifier Polytope for Expansion.} Concretely, let \(c=q_0\in\mathbb R^d\) denote the current robot position in the free space \(\mathcal{Q}\) and let
\(\{q_t\}_{t=0}^{H}\) be a candidate local trajectory submitted to verifier, with
\(p_t:=q_t-c\).  For a barrier parameter \(\gamma\in(0,1]\), define
\(\alpha_t=(1-\gamma)^t\) and \(\beta_t=1-\alpha_t\). We then formulate under each sensed circular obstacle that is represented as \(\mathcal O_i=\{x\in\mathbb R^d:\|x-o_i\|_2\le r_i\}\) where \(o_i\) and \(r_i\) are its center and radius. Instead of fixing the obstacle face normal, we seek to find a variable face \(\mathbf{a}_i^\top(x-c)\le m_i,\), that respects receding horizon safety \eqref{eq:poly}, where \(a_i\in\mathbb R^d\) is the verifier face normal and \(m_i\in\mathbb R\)
is its robot-centered margin. The optimization problem is solved for each nearby obstacle within the sensing region:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\max_{\ai, m_i}\ & m_i\\
\text{s.t. }\ & \ai^\top(\mathbf{q}_t-c)\le\big(1-(1-\gamma)^t\big)m_i \quad (t=1,\dots,H),\\
& r_i\|\ai\|_2\le \ai^\top(o_i-c)-m_i,\\
&\|\ai\|_2\le1,\ m_i\ge m_{\min}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:socp}
\end{equation}

The first constraint is the safety specification
\(h^{\text{poly}}(\xki;\xk)\geq \alpha_t\) and the second constraint is the separation since
\(\min_{x\in\mathcal O_i}\ai^\top(x-c)
=\ai^\top(o_i-c)-r_i\|\ai\|_2\). The third and fourth constraint is the normalization bound and for specifying minimum margin. After solving all per-obstacle
faces, the verifier polytope has the form \footnote{In order to guarantee the bounded verifier polytope in the sensing region, we solve \eqref{eq:socp} for auxiliary obstacles that is tangent to the largest K-sided polytope; K = 16 is used.}
\[
\mathcal P
=
\bigcap_i
\{x:\ai^\top(x-c)\le m_i\},
\]
% or equivalently \(Ax\le b\) with \(A_i=a_i^\top\) and
% \(b_i=a_i^\top c+m_i\). 
The objective is linear, the trajectory constraints are affine, and the obstacle-separation and normalization constraints are second-order-cone representable; hence the problem is an SOCP. In this way, the validity of sampled control policies is evaluated by the existence of the verifier polytope in terms of multi-step safety, \textit{i.e., $v(\mathbf{\hat u}) =1$}.

\begin{proposition} \label{prop: 3} A stochastic policy $\mathbf{u}_{k:k+H-1\mid k}$ with length $H$ produced by MPPI-DCBF~\eqref{eq:mppi-dcbf}, which is the constrained stochastic optimization problem solution under the outward normal $n$ from \eqref{eq:aff_barrier}, is a valid multi-step sample, \textit{i.e.,} \(v(\mathbf{u}_{k:k+H-1\mid k)} =1.\) Therefore, the nominal-polytope certificate induces a feasible face of the SOCP verifier.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Safe Flow Expansion}
Generative policies constrain actions to the distribution of their demonstrations. Their behavior is usually limited to the dominant modes present in the demonstration data. This becomes problematic in OOD scenarios, where the model encounters states unseen during training. To address this distribution shift, recent large-scale imitation learning frameworks attempt to gather massive amounts of human intervention data to fill the coverage gaps. However, data collection is inherently unscalable for capturing all possible safety-critical actions. To this end, we can We instead use the current generative policy to propose candidate action windows, query their validity with the deterministic verifier, and retrain on the resulting verified data~\cite{de2026active}. 

Concretely, for a given state-observation context \(\xi\) and safety parameter \(\gamma\), the policy at expansion round \(n\) induces the \textit{conditional generable set} associated with the probability density lower bound \(\tau\) defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_{\theta_n}^{\tau}(\xi,\gamma)
    :=
    \left\{
    \hat{\mathbf u}\in\mathcal U^H:
    p_1^{\theta_n}(\hat{\mathbf u}\mid\xi,\gamma)\ge\tau
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:conditional-generable-set}
\end{equation}
Here we abbreviate the \(H\) steps of predicted actions
\(\hat{\mathbf u}_{k:k+H-1}\) as \(\hat{\mathbf u}\). The pre-trained generable set is recovered by setting \(n=0\). The \textit{valid action set} is defined as the action certified multi-step safety of~\eqref{eq:socp}
\begin{equation}
    \Omega^\star(\xi,\gamma)
    :=
    \left\{
    \hat{\mathbf u}\in\mathcal U^H:
    v_\gamma(\hat{\mathbf u};\xi)=1
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:verifier-certified-set}
\end{equation}
The conditional generable set represents the actions the policy is likely given $\tau$, whereas the valid action set denotes the actions formally certified as safe. By Proposition~\ref{prop: 3}, every MPPI-DCBF receding horizon belongs to \(\Omega^\star(\xi,\gamma)\). When the pre-trained policy represents only a few dominant modes, its
generable set may cover only a small portion of the certified set,
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Vol}\!\left(
    \Omega_{\theta_0}^{\tau}(\xi,\gamma)
    \right)
    \ll
    \operatorname{Vol}\!\left(\Omega^\star(\xi,\gamma)\right).
\end{equation}
The valid OOD region targeted by expansion is therefore
\[
    \Omega^\star(\xi,\gamma)
    \setminus
    \Omega_{\theta_0}^{\tau}(\xi,\gamma).
\]

Algorithm~\ref{alg:sfe} implements data acquisition on verified actions under the evolving CFM policy. At each receding-horizon step, the current policy proposes uncertainty-tilted actions, and among those, the verifier \(v_{\gamma_k}\) is used to acquire safe actions. The data is collected in a receding-horizon manner, provided that \(\xi_k\) is a sufficient statistic for the system dynamics (\textit{i.e.,} input of the generative policy), this receding-horizon interaction induces a Markovian closed-loop sampling process. During the data acquisition, one can execute (the execution cost \(C\)) expert actions \cite{ross2011reduction} or restrict execution to a safe admissible action set \cite{alshiekh2018safe}. In our case, verifier validity remains enforced by restricting selection to \(\mathcal I_k^+\) and constructing this execution cost for efficient safe coverage remains an open question.

\textbf{Reachability Analysis.} Given the statistical model in~\eqref{eq:prob_valid_set}, binary feedback in the original action space $\mathcal U^H$ is given as \( \rho_\gamma(\hat{\mathbf u})=s\!\left(g(\phi_s(\hat{\mathbf u};\xi,\gamma))\right)\). For \(\epsilon>0\), define the one-step reachable operator as:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
R_\epsilon(S)
&:=
\Bigl\{
\hat{\mathbf u}\in\mathcal U^H:
\exists\hat{\mathbf u}'\in S\ \text{s.t.}\\[-1mm]
&\quad
\rho_\gamma(\hat{\mathbf u}')-\epsilon
-L_sB\,d_{\phi,k}^{\xi,\gamma}
(\hat{\mathbf u},\hat{\mathbf u}')\ge h
\Bigr\}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:reachable-action-set}
\end{equation}
Subsequently, we can define 
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
R_\epsilon^0(S)&:=S,\qquad
R_\epsilon^{j+1}(S):=R_\epsilon(R_\epsilon^j(S)),\\
R_\epsilon^{\le r}(S)&:=
\bigcup_{j=0}^{r}R_\epsilon^j(S).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cumulative-reachable-action-set}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Corollary~4.2~\cite{de2026active}]
\label{lem:reachable-generable-coverage}
The following is its conditional action-space adaptation. Fix \(r\ge1\)
and let \(T^\star=rT\) be verifier queries. Under the
assumptions in Appendix~\ref{sec:safe-flow-expansion-analysis} (TODO), with probability at least \(1-\delta\),
\begin{equation}
R_\epsilon^r(S_0)
\subseteq
R_\epsilon^{\le r}(S_0)
\subseteq
S_{T^\star}
\subseteq
\Omega_{\theta_{T^\star}}^\tau(\xi,\gamma).
\label{eq:main_inclusion}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

Define the coverage event and its deterministic verifier-safe part as
\begin{align}
\mathcal E_{\mathrm{cov}}
&:=
\left\{
R_\epsilon^{\le r}(S_0)
\subseteq S_{T^\star}
\subseteq\Omega_{\theta_{T^\star}}^\tau(\xi,\gamma)
\right\},
\label{eq:coverage-event}\\
A_{\gamma,r}(\xi)
&:=
R_\epsilon^{\le r}(S_0)
\cap\Omega^\star(\xi,\gamma).
\label{eq:safe-reachable-region}
\end{align}
On \(\mathcal E_{\mathrm{cov}}\),
\begin{equation}
A_{\gamma,r}(\xi)
\subseteq
\Omega_{\theta_{T^\star}}^\tau(\xi,\gamma)
\cap\Omega^\star(\xi,\gamma).
\label{eq:safe-generable-inclusion}
\end{equation}
Moreover, Assumption~\ref{ass:positive-volume-seed} gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
m_\gamma(\xi)
&:=
\operatorname{Vol}\!\left(A_{\gamma,r}(\xi)\right)\\
&\ge\operatorname{Vol}(S_0)>0.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:positive-reachable-volume}
\end{equation}


Having defined the conditional generable set~\eqref{eq:conditional-generable-set} and the verifier-valid action set~\eqref{eq:representation-valid-certified-set}, and the rechable operator~\eqref{eq:reachable-action-set}, we now state the main theorem on how set coverage via the expansion mechanism translates into the probability of obtaining a safe action window under a finite parallel sampling budget. The result separates the high-probability coverage guarantee over the expansion process from the deployment-time probability induced by sampling the learned policy. 

\begin{theorem}[Availability of a multi-step safe action]
\label{thm:safe-action-availability}
Assume the conditions of Lemma~\ref{lem:reachable-generable-coverage} and Lemma~\ref{thm:kernelized-logistic-confidence}, with $m_\gamma(\xi):=\operatorname{Vol}\!\left(R_\epsilon^r(S_0)\cap\Omega^\star(\xi,\gamma)\right)>0.$
After the safe flow expansion in
Lemma~\ref{lem:reachable-generable-coverage}, draw \(K\)
conditionally independent samples $\hat{\mathbf u}^{(1:K)}
\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}p_1^{\theta_{\hat n}}(\cdot\mid\xi,\gamma)$. Then conditioned on the \(1-\delta\) coverage event in Lemma~\ref{thm:kernelized-logistic-confidence},
\begin{align}
&\Pr\!\left(
\exists j\le K:
v_\gamma(\hat{\mathbf u}^{(j)};\xi)=1
\right)\\
&\ge
1-
\left(1-\tau m_\gamma(\xi)\right)^K
\ge
1-\exp\!\left(-K\tau m_\gamma(\xi)\right).
\label{eq:safe-action-hit-probability}
\end{align}
Consequently, over both expansion and generation randomness the probability of coverage holds and at least one action being safe is $(1-\delta)\left[1-\left(1-\tau m_\gamma(\xi)\right)^K\right].$
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
On the coverage event~\eqref{eq:reachable-generable-inclusion}, it holds that $
R_\epsilon^r(S_0)
\subseteq
\Omega_{\theta_{\hat n}}^\tau(\xi,\gamma).
$
Since the probability density lower bound $\tau$ applies to the entire volume $m_\gamma(\xi)$ under the coverage event, hence
\[
\Pr\!\left(
\hat{\mathbf u}\in
R_\epsilon^r(S_0)\cap\Omega^\star(\xi,\gamma)
\right)
\ge
\tau m_\gamma(\xi).
\]
Conditional independence gives the complement probability in
\eqref{eq:safe-action-hit-probability}. Every sample in
\(\Omega^\star(\xi,\gamma)\) passes the deterministic SOCP verifier
and is therefore multi-step safe.
\end{proof}


\noindent \textbf{Baseline method} We adapt CFM-MPPI~\cite{mizuta2025unified}, which combines reward-guided flow generation with sampling-based MPPI refinement. Given a base pre-trained vector field $\mathbf{v}^{\theta_{\text{0}}}(\mathbf{x}\!\mid\! \mathbf{o},\gamma)$, the guided velocity field is the local gradient ascent of the combined reward:
\begin{equation}
   \tilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\theta_{\text{0}}}(\mathbf{x}_\tau\!\mid\! \mathbf{o},\gamma)  = \mathbf{v}^{\theta_{\text{0}}}(\mathbf{x}_\tau\!\mid\! \mathbf{o},\gamma) \;+\; \eta \nabla_{\mathbf{z}_1}R(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}_1),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{\hat u} =\mathbf{z}_1 \sim p_1^{\theta_{\mathrm{0}}}(\cdot\mid\mathbf
o,\gamma)$ By integrating over $\tau$ from 0 to 1, the guided policy locally increases the cost that is designed to encourage both performance and safety.
\begin{equation}
    R(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\hat u}) = \sum_{k=0}^{H-1} w_\text{s} r_\text{s}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\hat u}) + w_\text{g} r_\text{g} (\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\hat u}) 
\end{equation}
The safety reward term is defined to discourage the CBF condition. While can be set arbitrarily, we set the performance reward term to be the same cost of the MPPI expert \eqref{eq:mppicost}. In our simulation studies, we evaluated possible pairs of both coefficients with the collision rate metric and deployed them during the evaluation

At every receding-horizon step, CFM-MPPI generates 200 guided control
sequences of horizon $H=10$ from the pretrained model. The ten sequences
with the lowest MPPI cost are retained, and each is perturbed 200
times with Gaussian noise. MPPI cost-weighted averaging produces ten
refined sequences, from which the minimum-cost sequence is selected and
its first action is executed. At the next state, the selected sequence is
shifted by one step and used as a warm-start at the flow time $\tau = 0.75$ to generate the
next guided generation and refinement step.

\section{Conclusion}
% This paper presented a framework for creating safety-tunable generative control policies for safety-critical tasks. We introduced a novel MPPI-DCBF planner that uses DCBFs to generate a rich dataset of provably safe multi-modal trajectories for linear systems. By training a conditional flow matching model on this data, we produced a real-time policy that can modulate its safety and feasibility via a single parameter at inference time, without the need for online sampling. Our experiments on 2D and 3D systems validate the approach, demonstrating a practical method for deploying safe, adaptable, and high-performance learned controllers.
