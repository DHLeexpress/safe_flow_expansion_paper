\begin{abstract}
Sampling-based predictive control (SBPC), such as model predictive path integral control (MPPI), efficiently predicts receding-horizon action, while conditional flow matching (CFM) can learn these behaviors as a fast generative policy. However, the safety of individual MPPI rollouts does not generally imply the safety of their weighted average, and a policy pretrained on safe demonstrations may poorly represent safe behaviors in out-of-distribution (OOD) contexts. 
We address both problems through a discrete-time control barrier function (DCBF) condition, enforced conservatively to produce certified safe data and permissively to verify new behaviors. We first introduce MPPI-DCBF, which rejects unsafe samples within a convex safe region before averaging and preserves the executed next-state safety certificate under control-affine dynamics and the full predicted-horizon certificate for linear time-invariant systems.  We analyzed the safety and feasibility properties of MPPI-DCBF via a single parameter $\gamma$ that modulates receding-horizon DCBF conditions. Building on this certified planner, we then formulate an optimization problem that verifies an arbitrary CFM-generated action against the same multi-step safety conditions. \textit{Safe Flow Expansion}, an offline framework to expand the range of safe actions the CFM policy can generate by a verification and update process while retaining the multi-step safety conditions on $\gamma$. In unseen 2D and 3D environments, we show that our framework improves safety and coverage while reducing computation time compared to the baseline method.

Sampling-based predictive control (SBPC) has been gaining traction in the nonlinear optimal control community. Completely general optimal control approaches often suffer from computational complexity associated with solving stochastic Hamilton--Jacobi--Bellman equations backwards in time, commonly referred to as the curse of dimensionality. SBPC is an alternative to gradient-based model predictive control (MPC), where a simple sampling procedure is used in place of a sophisticated nonlinear optimizer \cite{posa2014direct, kurtz2023inverse, aydinoglu2024consensus}. Algorithms in this family include model predictive path integral control (MPPI) \cite{williams2016aggressive}, predictive sampling \cite{howell2022predictive}, and cross-entropy methods \cite{rubinstein1999cross}. MPPI, for example, circumvents computational complexity by employing forward sampling of stochastic dynamics combined with Monte Carlo approximation techniques \cite{williams2017model}. The optimal control is then computed as the exponentially weighted average of the sampled trajectories, enabling real-time implementation.
SBPC algorithms are particularly well-suited to recent advances in parallel computing architectures and parallel simulation \cite{mjx, Genesis, makoviychuk2021isaac}, enabling them to scale to complex problems like in-hand dexterous manipulation \cite{li2025drop}, legged locomotion \cite{xue2025full}, and more \cite{howell2022predictive}.

Despite these advantages, SBPCs such as MPPI inherently struggle with safety guarantees, such as collision avoidance.
Safety constraints are typically incorporated indirectly through penalty terms in the cost function by penalizing the collision~\cite{williams2017model, mohamed2023gp, yin2023shield}. However, relying solely on these penalties lacks explicit safety guarantees because the procedure relies on sampling and averaging. For example, failure modes in Fig.~\ref{fig:hero} occur when rollouts from different local safe regions are averaged (\textit{e.g.}, left and right mode).
The central technical question is therefore whether the safety certificate survives the weighted averaging used by MPPI. This limitation motivates our goal of first integrating safety-critical constraints explicitly into SBPC frameworks. To this end, we introduce MPPI-DCBF, which prevents such failure modes by restricting all nonzero-weight rollouts to a common convex nominal polytope before averaging. Furthermore, answer this question exactly for the executed next state under control-affine dynamics and for the entire predicted horizon under linear time-invariant dynamics.

A common method to ensure safety in collision-avoidance tasks is to employ a safety filter, which minimally modifies the output of a potentially unsafe nominal controller to guarantee constraint satisfaction \cite{ames2014cbf}. However, a simple safety filter applied \textit{only} to the control output cannot guarantee safety because control objectives and long-term safety criteria are tightly coupled and potentially conflicting. Previous work has used control Lyapunov functions (CLF) and control barrier functions (CBF), often with relaxation factors, to address this issue as an optimization problem that mediates safety and feasibility \cite{ames2016control, zeng2021enhancing}.
Recent work has encoded this concept through predictive safety filters that leverage predictive CBFs in layered control architectures \cite{compton2024learning}. However, in cluttered environments, this direct optimization often becomes non-convex and intractable.

This computational burden motivates a shift from online constrained optimization to learning-based methods, where complex planning is performed offline to curate datasets of safe, multi-modal behavior. Generative models, such as diffusion and flow matching (FM), can synthesize motion trajectories and action sequences from diverse, high-dimensional demonstrations~\cite{chi2023diffusion, kurtz2025generative}.  In motion planning and control, learned generative priors have been combined with inference-time guidance to adapt trajectories to planning objectives and environments~\cite{carvalho2023mpdiffusion}. 

Among these, conditional flow matching (CFM) has emerged as a promising alternative to diffusion-based modeling~\cite{chisari2024learning, nguyen2025flowmp, ye2024efficient}, offering competitive generation quality with efficient sampling~\cite{lipman2022flow}. CFM learns a transport from a simple base distribution to the conditional distribution of demonstrated action sequences. Although it can represent multiple output modes, its training targets are sampled from the demonstrations~\cite{kurtz2025generative}, so it primarily reproduces modes represented in those data. More broadly, generable set expansion enlarges the set of designs an FM model generates with non-negligible probability via uncertainty-aware self-generation and iterative verification and updating~\cite{de2026active}, to expand the range of behaviors the FM model can generate. \section{Preliminaries}
\subsection{Model Predictive Path Integral control}
In discrete time, at state $\xk\in\mathbb R^{n_x}$, MPPI optimizes a mean control sequence $\{ \uki \}_{i=0}^{H-1}$, where $H$ is the planning horizon.
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:mppi}
\begin{align}
\min_{\{\mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k}\}}
& \mathbb{E}\!\left[
\phi\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{k+N\mid k}\right)
\!+\!\!\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\Big(q(\mathbf{x}_{k+i\mid k})\!+\!\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k}^\top R\,\mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k}\Big)
\right], \label{eq:mppi-cost} \tag{1a}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
& \mathbf{x}_{k+i+1\mid k}=f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{k+i\mid k},\,\mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k}+\varepsilon_{k+i}\right), 
\label{eq:mppi-dyn}  \tag{1b}\\
& \mathbf{x}_{k+i\mid k} \in \mathcal{X}, \quad \mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k} \in \mathcal{U}, \label{eq:mppi-set1} \tag{1c} \\
& \mathbf{x}_{k\mid k}=\mathbf{x}_k, \quad \mathbf{x}_{k+H-1\mid k} \in \mathcal{X}_f,\qquad \label{eq:mppi-set2} \tag{1d}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Here, $N$ is the $q: \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is any running cost, $\phi : \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is a terminal cost, $\varepsilon_{k+i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\,\Sigma)$ is the control disturbance, $\Sigma$ is the control disturbance variance, $R \in \mathbb{R}^{n_u \times n_u}$ is the control cost weight.

% \begin{aligned}
% q(\mathbf{x})

% &\quad+\kappa_{\text{soft}}\,e^{-\alpha\,d_{\min}(\mathbf{x})}

The MPPI optimal control sequence is a path-integral approximation of reward-weighted average random variations, solved by Monte Carlo methods and centered on the optimal policy sequence from the previous time step \cite{williams2017model}. The unexecuted part of the sequence shifts by one time step and serves as the warm start for the next optimization. 

Let this warm start sequence from the previous time step be denoted by $\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{k+i\mid k}$. For rollout $m=1,\dots,M$ with perturbations $\varepsilon^{(m)}_{k+i}\!\sim\!\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)$, MPPI measures cost per rollout via forward sampling of random controls and states that can be expressed as
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
\mathbf{u}^{(m)}_{k+i\mid k}&=\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{k+i\mid k}+\varepsilon^{(m)}_{k+i},\label{eq:mppi_rollout}\\
\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+i+1\mid k}&=f\!\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+i\mid k},\,\mathbf{u}^{(m)}_{k+i\mid k}\bigr).
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Then, the cost for each rollout is
\begin{equation}
S^{(m)}\!=\!\phi\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+N\mid k}\bigr)
+\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\Big(q\,\!\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+i\mid k}\bigr)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{u}^{(m)\top}_{k+i\mid k} R\,\mathbf{u}^{(m)}_{k+i\mid k}\Big).
\label{eq:mppicost}
\end{equation}
Information-theoretic MPPI uses soft-min importance weights \cite{williams2017info}:
\begin{equation}
\omega_m:=\exp\!\left(-\frac{S^{(m)}-S_{\min}}{\lambda_{\mathrm M}}\right),\qquad S_{\min}:=\min_{\ell=1,\ldots,M}S^{(\ell)}.
\label{eq:mppi-weights}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_M >0$ is a temperature that controls how heavily the optimal trajectory is weighted, and $S_\text{min}$ is the minimum rollout cost, used to improve numerical stability. Then, for $i \in \{0, ..., N-1\}$, the normalized weights define the following Monte Carlo update of the mean control sequence:
\begin{equation}
\Delta \mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k}=
\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\omega_m\,\varepsilon^{(m)}_{k+i}}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\omega_m},
\quad
\mathbf{u}^*_{k+i\mid k}\leftarrow \bar{\mathbf{u}}_{k+i\mid k}+\Delta \mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k}. \label{eq:mppi-update}
\end{equation}
Then the current optimal control $\mathbf{u}^*_{k\mid k}$ is applied. 
% & h^{\mathrm{aff}}\!\left(x_{k+i\mid k};\,x_{k\mid k}\right)\ \ge\ (1-\gamma)^i,\quad i=0,\dots,N. \tag{1d}\label{eq:safemppi}
This parallelizable sampling process makes MPPI well-suited to solving non-convex optimal control problems on modern GPU hardware. However, a key challenge arises in safety-critical applications. When obstacles are present, an obstacle-avoidance cost does not provide formal safety guarantees, as the final control is averaged over many trajectories. 

Several works have focused on incorporating safety into the forward sampling process. Approaches that shape the rollout distribution or impose barrier constraints to bias the sampling toward safety \cite{yin2022,yin2023shield}, but often lack a safety certificate. Conversely, methods that act as sampling-based safety filters by greedily selecting safe samples \cite{rabiee2024guaranteed} enforce safety but bypass the averaging process in \eqref{eq:mppi-update}, thereby sacrificing the optimality certificate.

\subsection{Conditional Flow Matching for trajectory generation}
Conditional Flow matching is increasingly used in robotics for trajectory generation and policy generation \cite{kurtz2025generative,mizuta2025unified}. Trajectory generation learns a mapping from noise to entire collision-free trajectories via end-to-end supervised learning, but it can struggle to generalize to out-of-distribution environments and may require complete retraining \cite{ye2024efficient, dai2025safefm}. 
% In contrast, (conditional) flow matching-based policy generation is more flexible, focuses on learning a mapping to a lower-dimensional control action, conditioned on observations \cite{kurtz2025generative}.

Here, we describe the important concept of conditional flow matching. For collision avoidance, it is essential to generate a trajectory conditioned on geometric observations while leveraging offline flow matching training and the fast computation of SBPC. To achieve this, the generative model predicts actions conditioned on the given observation $\mathbf{o_t}$ and executes for a short horizon \cite{chi2023diffusion}. 

Concretely, at the current discrete time step $k$, the model takes $O$ steps of observations $\mathbf{o}_{k:k+O-1}$, predicts $H$ steps of actions $\mathbf{\hat u}_{k:k+H-1}$, execute $A$ steps of predicted actions $\mathbf{\hat u}_{k:k+A-1}$. Here, $O$ is the observation horizon, $H$ is the planning horizon, $A$ is the execution horizon of the action. Throughout our experiments, we used fixed values of $O = 1, H = 10, A = 1$. 

At planning step $k$, let $\xi_k$ denote the available planning context, including the current state or state estimate, goal, and sensed obstacle geometry. The policy conditions on the observation history $o_{k-O+1:k}\subset\xi_k$, generates the action window $\hat{\mathbf u}_k:=\hat u_{k:k+H-1}\in\mathcal U^H\subset\mathbb R^{Hn_u}$, and executes its first $A$ actions before replanning; throughout the experiments, $O=1$, $H=10$, and $A=1$. The CFM model learns the distribution of expert action windows, so we write $z:=\mathbf u^\star_{k:k+H-1|k}\in\mathbb R^{Hn_u}$ rather than identifying $z$ with the single action $u^\star_{k|k}$.

Our framework uses a safety-critical MPPI planner as a pre-trained model and \textit{distills its safety} into conditional flow matching. Here, $\mathbf{v}_t(\cdot)$ denotes the flow matching vector field that is learned to regress the behavior of the planner. We use \(\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_u}\) for a target optimal control policy, \textit{i.e.,} the symbol \(\mathbf{u}^*_{k\mid k}\) used in the previous section.

Conditional Flow matching aims to learn a marginal vector field $\mathbf{v}^*_t(\x)$ that converts a simple distribution $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$ into a complex data distribution $z \sim p_{data}$. This is done by considering the marginal probability path $p_t(\x)$ that evolves from $\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$ to $p_{data}$. With an optimal transport probability path that drives noise $\epsilon$ to $\mathbf{z}$ with constant direction and speed in time~\cite{lipman2022flow}, the conditional flow matching (CFM) model $\mathbf{v}_t^\theta(\x)$ learns the conditional vector field $\mathbf{v}^*_t(x|\mathbf{z})$ per sampled point $x$ from conditional vector field by minimizing the mean-squared loss:
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}(\theta)&=
\mathbb{E}_{\substack{
t\sim \mathrm{Unif}[0,1],\\
\mathbf{z}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}},\\ \mathbf{x}\sim p_t(\cdot\mid z)
}} \notag
\!\Bigl[\bigl\|\mathbf{v}_t^\theta(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{v}_t^*(\mathbf{x}\mid \mathbf{z})\bigr\|^2\Bigr]
\label{eq:cfm}\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{\substack{
t\sim \mathrm{Unif}[0,1],\\ \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_d),\\
\mathbf{z}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}
}}
\!\Bigl[\bigl\|\mathbf{v}_t^\theta(t\mathbf{z}+(1-t)\epsilon)-(\mathbf{z}-\epsilon)\bigr\|^2\Bigr].
\end{align}

To generate a policy using the CFM model, we first assume there is a ground-truth marginal vector field that transforms noise into an optimal policy given observations. In our case, we view the task of generating safe robot motion trajectories conditioned on local geometric observations and states $\mathbf{o}$, a safety parameter $\gamma$. In this case, we need to expand the expectation from \eqref{eq:cfm} over all possible $o$, $\gamma$, and $c$. Thus, we define our training objective as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta)\!=\!
\mathbb{E}_{\substack{
t\sim \mathrm{Unif}[0,1],\\ \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_d),\\
(z,o,\gamma)\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}(\z,o,\gamma)
}}
\!\!\!\!\Bigl[\bigl\|\mathbf{v}_t^\theta(t\z+(1-t)\epsilon\!\mid\! o,\gamma)-(\z-\epsilon)\bigr\|^2\Bigr].
\label{eq:cfm_cost}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Discrete-Time Control Barrier Functions}
Let $\mathcal{C}=\{\x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_x}:\; h(\x)\ge 0\}$ denote the safe set for a continuously differentiable $h$.
For the discrete‐time control‐affine system
\begin{equation}
    \x_{k+1}=f(\x_k)+g(\x_k)\u_k, \label{eq:affinesystem}
\end{equation}
a function $h$ is a discrete‐time control barrier function (DCBF) associated with the control system \eqref{eq:affinesystem} if there exists $\gamma\in(0,1]$ such that for all $\x_k$ there exists a control $\u_k$ with
\begin{equation}
    \Delta h(\x_k,\u_k)\;:=\;h(\x_{k+1})-h(\x_k)\;\ge\;-\gamma\,h(\x_k).
    \label{eq:dtcbf}
\end{equation}
Condition \eqref{eq:dtcbf} implies the one‐step decay bound
\begin{equation*}
    h(\x_{k+1})\;\ge\;(1-\gamma)\,h(\x_k)
    \, \Longrightarrow \,
    h(\x_k)\;\ge\;(1-\gamma)^{k}\,h(\x_0),
    \label{eq:exp_decay}
\end{equation*}
\textit{i.e.,} the lower bound on the barrier value decays exponentially with base $(1-\gamma)$, ensuring forward invariance of $\mathcal{C}$ \cite{ames2014cbf,agrawal2017discrete}.

We first define the safety specification derived from a supporting hyperplane to the single obstacle. Let \(\piq:\mathbb R^{n_x} \to \mathbb R^d\) be the projection that maps robot states into the robot position. \(\piq(\xki)=\qki\in\mathbb R^d\) denote the robot position at state \(\mathbf{x}_{k+i \mid k}\) at discrete time step $k$ and horizon $i = 1, ..., H$. For each convex configuration-space obstacle $j$, let $\p_j$ be the point on its boundary closest to the current position $\qk$, with outward normal $\n_j$. The affine barrier function is defined as
\begin{equation}
h_j^{\mathrm{aff}}(\x;\xk) :=
\frac{\n_j^\top(\q-\p)}{\n_j^\top(\q_{k\mid k}-\mathbf{p})}.
\label{eq:aff_barrier}
\end{equation}
Since $\n_j^\top(\qk-\p_j)>0$, the safe set for each horizon $i$ can be defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{aff}}_{i,j}=\{\x \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \mid h_j^{\mathrm{aff}}(\x;\xk)\ge (1-\gamma)^i\},\label{eq:aff_safeset}
\end{equation}
is a convex half-space. Iterating the condition \eqref{eq:dtcbf} from $i=0$ with the normalization $h_j^{\mathrm{aff}}(\xk;\xk)=1$ yields
\begin{equation}
h_j^{\mathrm{aff}}(\xki;\xk) \ge (1-\gamma)^i. \quad i = 1, ..., H.
\label{eq:safetyspecification}
\end{equation}

\noindent \textbf{Nominal Polytope for MPPI safety.} We further define the notion of a \textit{nominal polytope} to generalize the safety definition to multiple obstacles. Let $\mathcal O(\xk)$ index the obstacles observed at current robot state. We define the nominal ego-centric (\textit{i.e.}, defined as a function of $\xk$) safety function
\begin{equation}
    h^{\mathrm{poly}}(\x;\xk) = \min_{j\in\mathcal O(\xk)} h^{\mathrm{aff}}_j(\x;\x_{k\mid k})
\end{equation}
Accordingly, the safe set for each horizon $i=1,\cdots,H$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{poly}}_i=\{\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \mid h^{\mathrm{poly}}(\x;\xk)\ge (1-\gamma)^i\},\label{eq:poly_safeset}
\end{equation}
Thus, despite the complete free space around multiple obstacles is generally nonconvex, $\mathcal C_i^{\mathrm{poly}}$ is a conservative convex inner approximation set obtained by intersecting half-spaces that separates current robot position and the obstacles. 

While the proper design of terminal ingredients (\textit{i.e.,} a terminal set and cost) can formally guarantee recursive feasibility in traditional MPC, the non-convex nature of the resulting optimization problem often makes real-time implementation computationally intractable \cite{rawlings2020model}. SBPC methods such as MPPI face similar challenges. Rather than proving recursive feasibility, our approach ensures safety by enforcing DCBF constraints at each time step, which relies on the weaker but more practical condition of pointwise feasibility, formalized in the following assumption.

\begin{assumption}[Pointwise feasibility]
\label{assumption_1}
For the discrete-time control affine system \eqref{eq:affinesystem},  for every replanning state $\xk$, and safety parameter $\gamma \in (0,1]$, 
there exists a control sequence\footnote{Assumption~\ref {assumption_1} is the condition for pointwise feasibility of the MPPI-DCBF algorithm at the current state; it ensures that the set of valid trajectories for the planner to find is non-empty.}
$\{ \uki \}_{i=0}^{H-1}\in\mathcal U^H$ such that the resulting trajectory $\{\xki\}_{i=1}^{H}$ lies within the sequence of safe sets defined in~\ref{eq:poly_safeset}, i.e., $\xki \in \mathcal{C}_i^{\mathrm{poly}}$ for all $i \in \{1, \dots, H\}$:
\begin{equation}
h^{\mathrm{poly}}(\xki;\xk)\ge(1-\gamma)^i,
\qquad i=1,\ldots,H.\label{eq:nominalpolytope}
\end{equation}
\end{assumption}

\section{Problem Definition}

To generate safety-critical policies, we first formulate our safe planning objective as a constrained optimization problem. To this end, consider $h(\cdot;\xk)$ to be any normalized ego-centric safety function whose nonnegative superlevel set represents modeled safe states at planning step $k$. We hold this function fixed over the prediction horizon and normalize it by $h(\xk;\xk)=1$. This general formulation is \textit{agnostic} to this choice of safety function:
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:sc-mppi}
\begin{align*}
\min_{\{\mathbf{u}_{k:k+H-1\mid k}\}}
& \mathbb{E}\!\left[J(\mathbf x_{k:k+H\mid k},\mathbf u_{k:k+H-1\mid k})
\right], \tag{1a} \label{eq:mppi-dcbf}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
& \eqref{eq:mppi-dyn}, \ \eqref{eq:mppi-set1}, \ \eqref{eq:mppi-set2},\\
& h(\xki;\xk)\ge (1-\gamma)^i. \tag{1e} \label{eq:poly}
\end{align*}
\end{subequations}
Direct optimization using a non-convex $h$ does not generally guarantee recursive feasibility for this problem and often makes real-time implementation computationally intractable~\cite{rawlings2020model}. On the other hand, from the view of set invariance, $\gamma$ modulates the trade-off between \textit{safety and feasibility}~\cite{zeng2021enhancing}. For example, a small $\gamma$ restricts the safety function to higher values, at the cost of reduced feasibility.

\begin{remark} In order to prove $h$ is a valid DCBF, it is necessary to find a pointwise set of control values at any given state that is non-empty, with corresponding $\gamma$. To be compatible with the averaging in MPPI, we assumed that all rollouts \eqref{eq:mppi_rollout} assigned nonzero MPPI weight after rejection based on \eqref{eq:dtcbf}. In practice, we trained the generative policy only on feasible action sets that will be further elaborated upon.
\end{remark}

\section{Method}
Having presented the necessary background on model predictive path integral control, discrete-time barrier functions, and conditional flow matching, we now formulate the proposed framework. At a high level, our approach integrates a flow-matching generative model with a robust safety verifier. To achieve this, MPPI-DCBF first instantiates the generic safety function as a nominal polytope (\textit{i.e.}, $h=h^{\mathrm{poly}}$) as defined in our problem definition (\ref{eq:mppi-dcbf}). Subsequently, the verifier we introduce preserves the same safety parameter $\gamma$ while admitting a less conservative safety function that depends on the queried action window.

\subsection{MPPI-DCBF}
We first propose model predictive path integral control with DCBF-based sample rejection (MPPI-DCBF), which modifies the standard MPPI framework to formally enforce safety. The core of our method is the safety specification in \eqref{eq:poly}, which is designed to guarantee the forward invariance of the sequence of time-varying safe sets $\mathcal{C}_i^{\mathrm{poly}}$ defined in \eqref{eq:poly_safeset}. This is achieved by integrating the DCBF condition directly into the MPPI sampling step. We reject any sampled rollout that violates the condition; consequently, we compute the optimal control sequence \eqref{eq:mppi-update} as a convex combination of only rollouts certified to be safe. Under the pointwise feasibility assumption (Assumption.~\ref{assumption_1}), we can formally analyze the properties of the MPPI-DCBF averaging process.

\begin{proposition} \label{prop: 1}
    For the discrete-time control affine system \eqref{eq:affinesystem}, if all contributing rollouts $(m)$ satisfy $\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+1\mid k} \in \mathcal{C}_1^{\mathrm{poly}}$, then the averaged state $\mathbf{x}^*_{k+1|k}$ under the MPPI-DCBF control is also guaranteed to be in the safe set $\mathcal{C}_1^{\mathrm{poly}}$.
    
    % \begin{proof}
    % Under the control affine dynamics, the state $\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+1\mid k}$ in the next time step is an affine function of the control inputs. Since the admissible inputs are a convex polytope and the control update of MPPI is a convex combination of control perturbations~\eqref{eq:mppi-update}, the resulting state $\mathbf{x}^*_{k+1\mid k}$ is a convex combination of the rollout states:
    % \begin{align}
    % \mathbf{x}^*_{k+1|k} = \sum_m \hat{\omega}_m \mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+1|k}, \nonumber

    % where $\hat{\omega}_m \in \mathbb{R}_{> 0}$ and $\sum_m \hat{\omega}_m = 1$ are the importance weights derived from \eqref{eq:mppi-weights} after the rejection of unsafe samples. Moreover, since the safe set $\mathcal{C}_1^{\mathrm{poly}}$ is a convex set, any convex combination of points within it must also lie within the set. Thus, if $\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+1\mid k} \in \mathcal{C}_1^{\mathrm{poly}}$ for all $m$, it follows that $\mathbf{x}^*_{k+1|k} \in \mathcal{C}_1^{\mathrm{poly}}$.
    % \end{proof}
\end{proposition}

If the dynamics of the system of interest are linear time-invariant (LTI) as
\begin{equation}

\end{equation}
then we can make a similar statement about safety along the \textit{entire} trajectory, rather than just a single step in the MPPI averaging process.

\begin{proposition} \label{prop:2}
For the discrete LTI system \eqref{eq:lti} under the same pointwise feasibility assumption, if all contributing rollouts $(m)$ satisfy $\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+i\mid k} \in \mathcal{C}_i^{\mathrm{poly}}$ for all steps $i \in \{1, \dots, H\}$, then the entire averaged MPPI trajectory also remains within the sequence of safe sets, \textit{i.e.,} $\mathbf{x}^*_{k+i\mid k} \in \mathcal{C}_i^{\mathrm{poly}}$ for all $i$.
\end{proposition}

\noindent \textbf{Verifier Polytope for Expansion.}
The nominal polytope in \eqref{eq:poly_safeset} is a convex inner approximation imposed on all nonzero-weight MPPI rollouts, thereby enabling the convexity-based averaging argument. In a nonconvex free space, however, a single shared region may exclude collision-free rollouts in other local modes, even if that region is enlarged~\cite{deits2015computing}. Therefore, to expand a generative policy into these unexplored safe modes, it is important to preserve the finite-horizon DCBF conditions while reducing the conservatism of the convex inner approximation relative to the nominal polytope. Given an action window proposed by the generative policy, the verifier instead evaluates its induced rollout individually at the current receding-horizon step, allowing the separating faces to depend on the queried rollout.

For a queried action \(\hat{\mathbf u}\), let
\(\{\xki\}_{i=0}^{H}\) denotes the resulting trajectory. For each convex configuration-space obstacle \(j\in\mathcal O(\xk)\), represented as \(\mathcal O_j:=\{\q\in\mathbb R^d:\|\q-\oj\|_2\le r_j\},\)
the verifier constructs a separating hyperplane \(\mathbf a_j^\top(\mathbf q-\qk)\le m_j,\)
by solving the maximization problem
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\max_{\aj, m_j}\ & m_j\\
\text{s.t. }\ & \aj^\top(\qki-\qk)\le\big(1-(1-\gamma)^i\big)m_j \; (i=1,\dots,H),\\
& r_j\|\aj\|_2\le \aj^\top(\oj-\qk)-m_j,\\
&\|\aj\|_2\le1,\ 0< m_{\min} \le m_j. \label{eq:socp}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
This is a second-order cone program (SOCP) that can be solved efficiently using 'some' tools. Specifically, the first constraint imposes the safety specification in \eqref{eq:poly} with \(h(\xki;\xk)=1-\aj^\top(\qki-\qk)/m_j\) equal to 1 at the current position and is required to remain at least $(1-\gamma)^i$. The obstacle-separation constraint follows from
\[
\inf_{\q\in\mathcal O_j}\aj^\top(\q-\qk)
=
\aj^\top(\oj-\qk)-r_j\|\aj\|_2
\ge m_j.
\]
The remaining constraints fix the scale of the normal and impose the strictly positive margin $m_{\min}$. 
Let $(\aj^\star,m_j^\star)$ denote a solution of
\eqref{eq:socp} for obstacle $j$. The resulting verifier polytope \footnote{In implementation, we additionally include auxiliary bounding faces around the sensing region, making $\mathcal P$ bounded (e.g., 12 auxiliary faces is used in Fig.~\ref{fig:polytope}).} for a queried action is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal P(\hat{\mathbf u};\xi,\gamma):=\bigcap_{j\in\mathcal O(\xk)}
\left\{
\q\in\mathbb R^d:
{\aj^\star}^{\!\top}(\q-\qk)\le m_j^\star
\right\}.
\label{eq:verifierpolytope}
\end{equation}

For brevity, we abbreviate the \(H\) steps of predicted control sequence (or action) as $\{ \hat{\u}_{k+i\mid k}\}_{i=0}^{H-1}$ as \(\hat{\mathbf u}\). Having formulated the optimization problem to verify the existence of a multi-step safety specification, we formally define the \textit{validity} of a queried control policy.
\begin{definition}
Let \(v_\gamma(\hat{\mathbf u};\xi) \in \{0, 1\}\) be a indicator function that returns \(1\) if the optimization problem~\eqref{eq:socp} is feasible for action \(\hat{\mathbf u}\) under context \(\xi\) and safety parameter \(\gamma\), and \(0\) otherwise. The \textit{valid action set} is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \Omega^\star(\xi,\gamma)
    :=
    \left\{
    \hat{\mathbf u}\in\mathcal U^H:
    v_\gamma(\hat{\mathbf u};\xi)=1
    \right\},
    \label{eq:verifier-certified-set}
\end{equation}
and action \(\hat{\mathbf u} \in \Omega^\star(\xi,\gamma)\) is a \textit{valid} action that guarantees the existence of a verifier polytope~\eqref{eq:verifierpolytope}.
\label{def:verifier}
\end{definition}

With the valid action set established, a natural question would be 'are the nominal safe trajectories generated by the MPPI-DCBF planner guaranteed to be valid under this verifier?' The following proposition formally confirms this relationship.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:nominal-verifier}
Fix $(\xi,\gamma)$ and suppose the configuration-space
obstacles are represented as above. Let $\p_j$ and the
outward unit normal $\n_j$ define the nominal face, and let \(d_j:=\n_j^\top(\qk-\p_j)=\|\qk-\oj\|_2-r_j>0.\)
If $m_{\min}\le\min_{j\in\mathcal O(\xk)}d_j$, then every \textit{feasible} action whose resulting trajectory satisfies
\[
h^{\mathrm{poly}}(\xki;\xk)\ge(1-\gamma)^i,
\qquad i=1,\ldots,H,
\]
belongs to $\Omega^\star(\xi,\gamma)$.
\end{proposition}

Consequently, every MPPI-DCBF rollout is a valid action whenever Assumption~\ref{assumption_1} holds. 
% {\color{blue} MJ: Natural question reviewer will ask: Why didn't you just use verifier polytope instead of nominal polytope for the MPPI? - might need a simple sentence to answer that}
In particular, this follows under the LTI conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop:2}, while a general control-affine averaged window must be verified separately.
Furthermore, we can formalize how the safety parameter $\gamma$ explicitly modulates the valid action set. As demonstrated in the following proposition, higher values of $\gamma$ systematically relax the safety threshold.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:gamma}
For any fixed context $\xi$ and
$0<\gamma_1\le\gamma_2\le1$,
\begin{equation}
\Omega^\star(\xi,\gamma_1)
\subseteq
\Omega^\star(\xi,\gamma_2).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\noindent\emph{Proof.}
For every horizon index $i$,
$1-(1-\gamma)^i$ is nondecreasing in $\gamma$.
Thus, any valid action at $\gamma_1$ also satisfies the trajectory constraints at $\gamma_2$, while all remaining constraints in~\eqref{eq:socp} are unchanged. \hfill$\blacksquare$

In the context of offline training, generative policies constrain actions to the distribution of their demonstrations. While Proposition~\ref{prop:nominal-verifier} formally guarantees that the MPPI-DCBF planner allows us to produce rapid multi-step safe (valid) action priors, relying solely on these demonstrations inherently yields a subset of the distribution of all possible safe actions at given context and required safety level (\textit{e.g.,} $\gamma$). This fundamental limitation strongly motivates us to expand the generative policy distribution beyond the initial demonstrations to discover a broader range of valid actions.

\subsection{Safe Flow Expansion}
We introduce Safe Flow Expansion (Alg.~\ref{alg:sfe}) for the CFM model, which acquires missing safe behavioral modes by first pretraining on that safe action support and iteratively increasing the probability mass on uncertain multi-step safe actions.
% This becomes problematic in OOD scenarios, where the model encounters states unseen during training. To address this distribution shift, recent large-scale imitation learning frameworks attempt to gather massive amounts of human intervention data to fill the coverage gaps \cite{}. However, data collection is inherently unscalable for capturing all possible safety-critical actions. To this end, we instead use the current generative policy to propose candidate action windows, query their validity with the deterministic verifier, and retrain on the resulting verified data~\cite{de2026active}. 

Concretely, for a given state-observation context \(\xi\) and safety parameter \(\gamma\), the policy at expansion round \(n\) induces the \textit{conditional generable set} associated with the probability density lower bound \(\tau\) defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_{\theta_n}^{\tau}(\xi,\gamma)
    :=
    \left\{
    \hat{\mathbf u}\in\mathcal U^H:
    p_1^{\theta_n}(\hat{\mathbf u}\mid\xi,\gamma)\ge\tau
    \right\},
    \label{eq:conditional-generable-set}
\end{equation}
where $p_1^{\theta_n}(\mathbf u\mid\xi,\gamma)$ denotes the conditional policy density at round $n$.

The conditional generable set represents the actions the policy is likely valid given $\tau$. Here, we recover the pre-trained conditional generable set by setting \(n=0\).

To self-generate samples informative about multi-step safety while staying near the current policy \(\theta_n\), at expansion round \(n\),
we sample
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf u}
\sim
\frac{
p_1^{\theta_n}(\hat{\mathbf u}\mid\xi,\gamma)
\exp\!\left(
\sigma_n\!\left(\phi_s(\hat{\mathbf u};\xi,\gamma)\right)/\beta
\right)
}{
Z_n(\xi,\gamma)
},
\label{eq:importancesampling}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
Z_n(\xi,\gamma)
=
\int_{\mathcal U^H}
p_1^{\theta_n}(\mathbf u\mid\xi,\gamma)
\exp\!\left(
\sigma_n\!\left(\phi_s(\mathbf u;\xi,\gamma)\right)/\beta
\right)
d\mathbf u,
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_n$ is the kernel uncertainty returned by
\textsc{UpdateUncertainty} in Alg.~\ref{alg:sfe} and $\phi_s$ denotes the penultimate activation of the conditional flow vector field evaluated at the fixed flow-noise level $s$.

At each replanning step $k$, SFE self-generates a finite batch from the current CFM policy using the verifier uncertainty. The verifier then deterministically separates the queried actions \(\hat{\mathbf u}^{(j)}\) into valid (\textit{i.e., \(j\in\mathcal J^+_k\)}) and invalid sets. The execution cost \(C\) here does not define validity but only ranks verifier-positive candidates among the $B$ uncertain candidates, from which the minimum-cost first action is executed. During data acquisition, one can set this cost as expert actions \cite{ross2011reduction} or restrict execution to a safe admissible action set \cite{alshiekh2018safe}. In our case, we set the cost the same as the MPPI cost; additionally, for the dynamic environments, we used the constant velocity model to incorporate future collision cost. \subsection{Safe Flow Expansion analysis}
To characterize when Safe Flow Expansion can extend the conditional generable set~\ref{eq:conditional-generable-set} toward previously uncovered valid actions, we formulate the analysis in the way that SFE evaluates verifier uncertainty on the noised representation $\phi_s(\hat{\mathbf u};\xi,\gamma)$ rather than directly on the raw action space. This representation can assign high similarity to two actions that induce similar policy behaviors even when they are distant in the Euclidean space of $\mathcal U^H$. At the same time, both the conditional policy density $p_1^{\theta}(\hat{\mathbf u}\mid\xi,\gamma)$ and the deterministic valid action set~\ref{eq:verifier-certified-set} are defined in the original action space. We therefore preserve $\mathcal U^H$ as the analysis domain and construct the kernel using the learned feature map $\phi_s$. 

Concretely, for a fixed context $\xi$ and a safety parameter $\gamma$, we can construct a frozen feature kernel 
\begin{equation}
    k_{\phi}^{\xi,\gamma}(\hat{\mathbf u},\hat{\mathbf u}'):= k(\phi_s(\hat{\mathbf u};\xi,\gamma),\phi_s(\hat{\mathbf u}';\xi,\gamma)).
\end{equation} 
For the following analysis, we freeze $\phi_s$ so that $k_{\phi}^{\xi,\gamma}$ remains fixed over the sequence of verified action queries, as required by the kernel confidence sequence that will be followed. 

We next introduce the stochastic Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) logistic feedback model used by
\cite[Thm.~F.1]{de2026active} as a statistical model to estimate the safety of unverified actions. The model is formulated within a noisy flow representation that induces the conditional representation space $\mathcal Z_{\xi,\gamma}:=\left\{\phi_s(\hat{\mathbf u};\xi,\gamma):\hat{\mathbf u}\in\mathcal U^H\right\}.$
Specifically, for a queried action
$\hat{\mathbf u}$, the bandits model~\cite{pasztor2024bandits} assumes at the fixed $(\xi,\gamma)$,
\begin{equation}
Y\mid\hat{\mathbf u}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}\!\left(\varsigma(g({\phi_s(\hat{\mathbf u};\xi,\gamma)}))\right),
\label{eq:logistic-model}
\end{equation}
and define
\begin{equation}
\Omega_*(\xi,\gamma) := \{z\in \mathcal Z_{\xi,\gamma} : \varsigma(g(z)) \ge h\}, \; h\in(0,1)
\label{eq:prob_valid_set}
\end{equation}
as a \textit{valid conditional representation set} ((\textit{c.f.}, $\Omega^*(\xi,\gamma)$ in~\ref{eq:verifier-certified-set}), where $\varsigma$ is a sigmoid function, $g\in\mathcal H_{k_\phi^{\xi,\gamma}}$,$\|g\|_{k_\phi^{\xi,\gamma}}\le B$. in contrast to the implemented verifier returns the deterministic label $v_\gamma(\hat{\mathbf u};\xi)$. Thus, the resulting confidence guarantee
is conditional on the logistic feedback model being well-specified (\textit{i.e.,} actions with the same representation must have the same probability above for a fixed \(g\) that has bounded RKHS norm).

At each expansion round, we collect valid actions from successful trajectories, and the uncertainty-estimation buffer collects i.i.d. sampled valid actions throughout the successful trajectory, along with the safety parameter.

\end{equation}

o,\gamma)$ By integrating over $\tau$ from 0 to 1, the guided policy locally increases the reward that is designed to encourage both performance and safety
\begin{equation}

\end{equation}
where $w_\text{s}, w_\text{g}$ are scalar weights. The safety reward term $r_\text{s}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\hat u})$ is defined to discourage the CBF condition, such as in \cite[Eq. 6]{mizuta2025unified}. While it can be set arbitrarily, we set the performance reward term $r_\text{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\hat u})$ to be the same as the prescribed execution cost for the expansion. In our simulation studies, we evaluated various combinations of the scalar weights and selected the pair that provided the best trade-off between safety (collision rate) and task performance (time-to-goal).

\subsection{3D Quadrotor: Hardware}

We demonstrated that the SFE increases model coverage when encountering OOD obstacle geometries. Since the MPPI-DCBF demonstrations are collected in environments with vertical cylinders that spans the entire altitude in the workspace, so the training data is heavily biased toward planar lateral modes therefore yield two dominant modes in pretrained model (Two left columns in Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage}(a)). The pretrained CFM carries this bias into the unseen five-sphere scene: although the spheres open additional free space above and below, its rollouts remain near the demonstration altitude and concentrate on a few lateral modes.

After one expansion round, the policy begins to enter interior gaps and vary its altitude, revealing modes absent from the cylindrical pretraining data. The final expanded policy covers distinct lateral, over, and under modes, with all displayed final-policy rollouts remaining collision-free. This progression shows that Safe Flow Expansion folds verifier-positive OOD actions back into training and shifts probability mass toward new safe behaviors, rather than only selecting among modes already represented by the pretrained policy.

While the baseline CFM-MPPI also broadens trajectory coverage, we observed that relying solely on online guidance frequently results in collisions, though we allowed the method to generate parallel guided actions. 
Specifically, CFM-MPPI used a \textbf{fixed pretrained generable set} and relied on an additive guidance term. In out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios the pretrained base flow $\mathbf{v}^{\theta_{\text{0}}}$ in~\ref{eq:cfm-mppi_vectorfield} inherently lacks knowledge of novel safe modes. This becomes problematic, especially in the case where $\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_1}R(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}_1)$ becomes negligible when guidance associated with reward and safety $r_s, r_g$ counteracts each other.

Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage}(b) compares online replanning latency for the deployed configurations, using 512 samples for both MPPI variants and NFE \(=16\) for both flow policies, and 32 guided proposals for CFM-MPPI. The MPPI variants are dominated by the same forward rollouts; enforcing the DCBF safety constraint adds only \(2.24\,\mathrm{ms}\), increasing latency from \(8.94\) to \(11.18\,\mathrm{ms}\), or \(25\%\). CFM-MPPI instead requires \(47.22\,\mathrm{ms}\), over \(4\times\) the latency of MPPI-DCBF, because it repeats reward-guided generation and MPPI refinement online. Approximately \(50\%\) of its runtime is spent on differentiable guidance and \(41\%\) on the subsequent 256-rollout refinement.

In contrast, the expanded CFM performs one context encoding and one unguided horizon-\(H\) generation, without online dynamics rollouts or verifier calls, and replans in \(0.93\,\mathrm{ms}\). This corresponds to a \(9.6\times\) speedup over MPPI and a \(50.8\times\) speedup over CFM-MPPI. Safe Flow Expansion therefore moves verifier-guided mode discovery offline and amortizes it into the policy, retaining the online efficiency of unguided generation.
\section{Conclusion}
A natural next step for Safe Flow Expansion is to scale the framework to systems with extremely large state spaces or where expert demonstrations are prohibitively expensive. In parallel, while our method robustly expands the safety distribution, tuning the online execution cost $C$ can still induce a distribution shift away from the expanded priors; addressing this objective mismatch remains an important challenge.

On the other hand, a limitation of our current generative model inherits safety statistically from the data and lacks its own formal certificate. While we rely on a single generative rollout to test the framework, we expect that parallelizing multiple generative priors can improve performance, especially in dynamic environments. Future work could explore combining our generative policy with a lightweight run-time safety filter to provide hard guarantees. 

Additionally, learning an optimal $\gamma$ schedule directly from data remains an interesting open problem (\textit{e.g.,} rather than a fixed $\gamma$ during deployment).

We introduced a novel MPPI-DCBF planner that uses DCBFs to generate a rich dataset of provably safe multi-modal trajectories for linear systems. By training a conditional flow matching model on this data, we produced a real-time policy that can modulate its safety and feasibility via a single parameter at inference time, without the need for online sampling. Our experiments on 2D and 3D systems validate the approach, demonstrating a practical method for deploying safe, adaptable, and high-performance learned controllers.
