\begin{abstract}
Sampling-based predictive control (SBPC) (e.g. model predictive path integral control (MPPI)) efficiently predicts receding-horizon actions.  However, the safety of individual MPPI rollouts does not generally imply the safety of their weighted average.  Moreover, policies pretrained on safe demonstrations may poorly represent safe behaviors in out-of-distribution (OOD) deployments. This paper introduces new methods to ensure safety in SBPC and 
to guarantee a conditional lower bound on probabilistic safety in OOD scenarios. 
%to guarantee probabilistic safety in OOD scenarios.  
We address sampling-based safety through a discrete-time control barrier function (DCBF) condition, enforced conservatively to produce certified safe data and to verify new behaviors.  We first introduce MPPI-DCBF, which rejects unsafe samples within a convex safe region before averaging and preserves the executed next-state safety certificate under control-affine dynamics and the full predicted-horizon certificate for linear time-invariant systems.  We analyze the safety and feasibility properties of MPPI-DCBF via a single parameter $\gamma$ that modulates receding-horizon DCBF conditions. Building on this certified planner, we then formulate an optimization problem that verifies an arbitrary CFM policy against the same multi-step safety conditions. \textit{Safe Flow Expansion} is an offline framework to expand the range of safe actions that the CFM policy can generate by a verification and update process while retaining the multi-step safety conditions on $\gamma$. In unseen 2D and 3D environments, we show that our framework improves safety and mode coverage while reducing computation time compared to the baseline method.
\end{abstract}

Sampling-based predictive control (SBPC) is gaining traction as an optimal control framework. Completely general optimal control approaches often suffer from the computational complexity that is associated with solving stochastic Hamilton--Jacobi--Bellman equations backward in time. SBPC is an alternative to gradient-based model predictive control (MPC), where a simple sampling procedure replaces a sophisticated nonlinear optimizer~\cite{posa2014direct, kurtz2023inverse, aydinoglu2024consensus}. Algorithms in this family include model predictive path integral control (MPPI)~\cite{williams2016aggressive}, predictive sampling~\cite{howell2022predictive}, Generative Predictive Control~\cite{Kurtz_GPC}, and cross-entropy methods~\cite{rubinstein1999cross}. MPPI, for example, circumvents computational complexity by employing forward sampling of stochastic dynamics combined with Monte Carlo approximation techniques~\cite{williams2017model}. The optimal control is then computed as the exponentially weighted average of the sampled trajectories, enabling real-time implementation. SBPC algorithms are particularly well-suited to recent advances in parallel computing architectures and parallel simulation~\cite{mjx, Genesis, makoviychuk2021isaac}, enabling them to scale to complex problems like in-hand dexterous manipulation~\cite{li2025drop}, legged locomotion~\cite{xue2025full}, and more~\cite{howell2022predictive, Kurtz_GPC}.

Despite these advantages, SBPCs such as MPPI, inherently struggle with safety guarantees, such as collision avoidance.  Safety constraints are typically incorporated indirectly through penalty terms in the path planning cost function~\cite{williams2017model, mohamed2023gp, yin2023shield}. While this approach encourages safe behavior, it lacks explicit safety guarantees because the procedure relies on sampling and averaging. For example, the failure in Fig.~\ref{fig:hero} occurs when rollouts from different local safe regions are averaged (\textit{e.g.}, left and right mode).  Generally, current safety certificates do not survive the weighted averaging used by MPPI. This limitation, which applies to all current SBPC methods, motivates our goal of first integrating safety-critical constraints explicitly into SBPC frameworks. To this end, we introduce MPPI-DCBF, which prevents such failure modes by restricting all nonzero-weight rollouts to a common convex nominal polytope before averaging.

A common method to ensure safety in collision-avoidance tasks is to use a safety filter~\cite{ames2014cbf}, which minimally modifies the nominal controller output to guarantee safety constraint satisfaction. However, a simple safety filter applied \textit{only} to the control output cannot guarantee safety because control objectives and long-term safety criteria are tightly coupled and potentially conflicting. Previous work has used control Lyapunov functions (CLF) and control barrier functions (CBF), often with relaxation factors, to address this issue as an optimization problem that mediates safety and feasibility~\cite{ames2016control, zeng2021enhancing}.
Recent work has encoded this concept through predictive safety filters that leverage predictive CBFs in layered control architectures~\cite{compton2024learning}. However, in cluttered environments, this direct optimization often becomes non-convex and intractable.

This computational burden motivates a shift from online constrained optimization to learning-based methods, where complex planning is performed offline to curate datasets of safe and multi-modal behavior. Generative models, such as diffusion and flow matching (FM)~\cite{lipman2022flow}, can synthesize motion trajectories and action sequences from diverse, high-dimensional demonstrations~\cite{chi2023diffusion, Kurtz_GPC}. Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) has emerged as a promising alternative to diffusion-based modeling, offering competitive generation quality with efficient sampling~\cite{chisari2024learning, nguyen2025flowmp, ye2024efficient}. CFM is particularly useful in safety-critical motion planning and control, as it can generate high-fidelity, multi-modal trajectories with fewer inference time steps. Maintaining this topological diversity is crucial to avoid committing to a single local solution that may become infeasible as the scene changes~\cite{degroot2024topology, shafiullah2022behavior}. However, they do not natively provide formal safety guarantees during rollout. 

Recent CFM-based works have further incorporated explicit safety constraints at inference time by trajectory regularization or constrained generation~\cite{li2026hardflow,utkarsh2026physics}, while MPC-based refinement has been used to improve local optimality of generated trajectories~\cite{mizuta2025unified}. However, these methods guide or refine samples from a fixed pretrained distribution. In OOD contexts, the pretrained model may assign low probability to \textbf{safe and multi-modal} behaviors, and aggressive guidance can further push generation away from its learned distribution~\cite{dandapanthula2026we}.

Consequently, a fundamental challenge in generative motion planning and control is to address this distribution shift by expanding the generable set of a \textbf{pretrained policy toward safe and multi-modal regions}. Recent work formalizes this as generable set expansion: enlarging the set of designs an FM model generates with non-negligible probability via uncertainty-aware self-generation and iterative verification and updating~\cite{de2026active}, to expand the range of behaviors the FM model can generate. We extend this concept to a CFM model to discover new safe behaviors in robotics motion planning. To this end, we introduce the \textit{Safe Flow Expansion} framework, which expands the pretrained policy toward new safe behaviors using a safety-critical predictive planner and verifier mechanisms.

\textbf{Contributions.} Our contributions are threefold:
(i) An MPPI-DCBF planner that rejects sampled rollouts violating finite-horizon DCBF conditions before weighted averaging, preserving the safety certificate of the executed next state under control-affine dynamics and the entire predicted horizon for LTI systems.
(ii) Safe Flow Expansion, an offline uncertainty-guided procedure that self-generates and verifies CFM actions in OOD environments, therefore expands the pretrained policy toward new safe behaviors. 
(iii) A closed-loop analysis and evaluation that carries the DCBF parameter of MPPI-DCBF through certified demonstration generation derives a conditional lower bound on probabilistic safety for a safety-tunable generative policy. Evaluations in 2D cluttered and pedestrian environments and on a 3D quadrotor show increased multi-step safe probability and recovery of safe trajectory modes in OOD environments.

\section{Preliminaries}
\subsection{Model Predictive Path Integral control}
In discrete time, at state $\xk\in\mathbb R^{n_x}$, MPPI optimizes a mean control sequence $\{ \uki \}_{i=0}^{H-1}$, where $H$ is the planning horizon.
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:mppi}
\begin{align}
\min_{\{\mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k}\}}
& \mathbb{E}\!\left[
\phi\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{k+H\mid k}\right)
\!+\!\!\sum_{i=0}^{H-1}\Big(q(\mathbf{x}_{k+i\mid k})\!+\!\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k}^\top R\,\mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k}\Big)
\right], \label{eq:mppi-cost} \tag{1a}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
& \mathbf{x}_{k+i+1\mid k}=f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{k+i\mid k},\,\mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k}+\varepsilon_{k+i}\right), 
\label{eq:mppi-dyn}  \tag{1b}\\
& \mathbf{x}_{k+i\mid k} \in \mathcal{X}, \quad \mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k} \in \mathcal{U}, \label{eq:mppi-set1} \tag{1c} \\
& \mathbf{x}_{k\mid k}=\mathbf{x}_k, \quad \mathbf{x}_{k+H-1\mid k} \in \mathcal{X}_f,\qquad \label{eq:mppi-set2} \tag{1d}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Here, $q: \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is a running cost, $\phi : \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is a terminal cost, $\varepsilon_{k+i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\,\Sigma)$ is the control disturbance, $\Sigma$ is the control disturbance variance, $R \in \mathbb{R}^{n_u \times n_u}$ is the control cost weight.
% For the collision avoidance task, the controller can reason about crashing by setting collision penalties using the signed clearance to the closest obstacle $d_{\min}(\mathbf{x})$ in their running cost function, such as
% \begin{equation}
% \begin{aligned}
% q(\mathbf{x})
% &=(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{goal}})^\top Q (\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{goal}})\\
% &\quad+\kappa_{\text{soft}}\,e^{-\alpha\,d_{\min}(\mathbf{x})}
% +\kappa_{\text{hard}}\,\mathbf{1}\!\bigl[d_{\min}(\mathbf{x})<0\bigr].
% \label{eq:mppisoftconstraint}
% \end{aligned}

The MPPI optimal control sequence is a path-integral approximation of reward-weighted average random variations, solved by Monte Carlo methods and centered on the optimal policy sequence from the previous time step \cite{williams2017model}. The unexecuted part of the sequence shifts by one time step and serves as the warm start for the next optimization. 

Let this warm start sequence from the previous time step be denoted by $\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{k+i\mid k}$. For rollout $m=1,\dots,M$ with perturbations $\varepsilon^{(m)}_{k+i}\!\sim\!\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)$, MPPI measures cost per rollout via forward sampling of random controls and states that can be expressed as
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
\mathbf{u}^{(m)}_{k+i\mid k}&=\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{k+i\mid k}+\varepsilon^{(m)}_{k+i},\label{eq:mppi_rollout}\\
\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+i+1\mid k}&=f\!\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+i\mid k},\,\mathbf{u}^{(m)}_{k+i\mid k}\bigr).
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Then, the cost for each rollout is
\begin{equation}
S^{(m)}\!=\!\phi\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+N\mid k}\bigr)
+\sum_{i=0}^{H-1}\Big(q\,\!\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+i\mid k}\bigr)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{u}^{(m)\top}_{k+i\mid k} R\,\mathbf{u}^{(m)}_{k+i\mid k}\Big).
\label{eq:mppicost}
\end{equation}
Information-theoretic MPPI uses soft-min importance weights \cite{williams2017info}:
\begin{equation}
\omega_m:=\exp\!\left(-\frac{S^{(m)}-S_{\min}}{\lambda_{\mathrm M}}\right),\qquad S_{\min}:=\min_{\ell=1,\ldots,M}S^{(\ell)}.
\label{eq:mppi-weights}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_M >0$ is a temperature that controls how heavily the optimal trajectory is weighted, and $S_\text{min}$ is the minimum rollout cost, used to improve numerical stability. Then, for $i \in \{0, ..., H-1\}$, the normalized weights define the following Monte Carlo update of the mean control sequence:
\begin{equation}
\Delta \mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k}=
\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\omega_m\,\varepsilon^{(m)}_{k+i}}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\omega_m},
\quad
\mathbf{u}^*_{k+i\mid k}\leftarrow \bar{\mathbf{u}}_{k+i\mid k}+\Delta \mathbf{u}_{k+i\mid k}. \label{eq:mppi-update}
\end{equation}
Then the current optimal control $\mathbf{u}^*_{k\mid k}$ is applied. 
% & h^{\mathrm{aff}}\!\left(x_{k+i\mid k};\,x_{k\mid k}\right)\ \ge\ (1-\gamma)^i,\quad i=0,\dots,N. \tag{1d}\label{eq:safemppi}
This parallelizable sampling process makes MPPI well-suited to solving non-convex optimal control problems on modern GPU hardware. However, a key challenge arises in safety-critical applications. When obstacles are present, an obstacle-avoidance cost does not provide formal safety guarantees, as the final control is averaged over many trajectories. 

Several works have focused on incorporating safety into the forward sampling process of MPPI. Approaches that shape the rollout distribution or impose barrier constraints to bias the sampling toward safety \cite{yin2022,yin2023shield} often lack a safety certificate. Conversely, methods that act as sampling-based safety filters by greedily selecting safe samples \cite{rabiee2024guaranteed} enforce safety but bypass the averaging process in \eqref{eq:mppi-update}, thereby bypassing the MPPI cost-weighted update.

\subsection{Conditional Flow Matching for trajectory generation}
Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) is increasingly used in robotics for trajectory generation and policy generation \cite{Kurtz_GPC,mizuta2025unified}. Trajectory generation learns a mapping from noise to entire collision-free trajectories via end-to-end supervised learning, but it can struggle to generalize to OOD environments and may require complete retraining \cite{ye2024efficient, dai2025safefm}.

Here, we describe the important concept of CFM. For collision avoidance, it is essential to generate a trajectory conditioned on geometric observations while leveraging offline flow matching training and the fast computation of SBPC. To achieve this, the generative model predicts actions conditioned on the given observation and executes for a short horizon \cite{chi2023diffusion}. 

Concretely, at the current discrete time step $k$, the model takes $O$ steps of observations $\mathbf{o}_{k-O+1:k}$, predicts $H$ steps of action sequence (hereafter abbreviated as actions) $\mathbf{\hat u}_{k:k+H-1}$, execute $A$ steps of predicted actions $\mathbf{\hat u}_{k:k+A-1}$. Here, $O$ is the observation horizon, $H$ is the planning horizon, and $A$ is the execution horizon of the action. Throughout our experiments, we used fixed values of $O=1$, $H=10$, and $A=1$. Our framework first uses an MPPI-DCBF as a safe action generative prior to pretrain the CFM model. Contingent on the previous subsection, the CFM model learns the distribution of expert actions, as we write $\z:=\mathbf u^\star_{k:k+H-1|k}\in\mathbb R^{H\times n_u}$. Here, $\mathbf{v}_t(\cdot)$ denotes the flow matching vector field that is learned to regress the behavior of the planner.

CFM aims to learn a marginal vector field $\mathbf{v}^*_t(\x)$ that converts a simple distribution $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$ into a complex data distribution $\z \sim p_{data}$. This is done by considering the marginal probability path $p_t(\x)$ that evolves from $\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$ to $p_{data}$. With an optimal transport probability path that drives noise $\epsilon$ to $\mathbf{z}$ with constant direction and speed in time~\cite{lipman2022flow}, the CFM model $\mathbf{v}_t^\theta(\x)$ learns the conditional vector field $\mathbf{v}^*_t(\x|\z)$ per sampled point $\x$ from conditional vector field by minimizing the mean-squared loss:
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}(\theta)&=
\mathbb{E}_{\substack{
t\sim \mathrm{Unif}[0,1],\\
\mathbf{z}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}},\\ \mathbf{x}\sim p_t(\cdot\mid \z)
}} \notag
\!\Bigl[\bigl\|\mathbf{v}_t^\theta(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{v}_t^\star(\mathbf{x}\mid \mathbf{z})\bigr\|^2\Bigr]
\label{eq:cfm}\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{\substack{
t\sim \mathrm{Unif}[0,1],\\ \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_d),\\
\mathbf{z}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}
}}
\!\Bigl[\bigl\|\mathbf{v}_t^\theta(t\mathbf{z}+(1-t)\epsilon)-(\mathbf{z}-\epsilon)\bigr\|^2\Bigr].
\end{align}

To generate a CFM policy, we assume a ground-truth marginal vector field that transforms noise into an optimal policy given observations. In our case, we view the task as generating safe robot motion trajectories conditioned on observation $\mathbf{o}$ and a safety parameter $\gamma$ (we denote this as the DCBF parameter or safety parameter); then we need to expand the expectation from \eqref{eq:cfm} over all possible $\o$ and $\gamma$. Thus, we define our training objective as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta)\!=\!
\mathbb{E}_{\substack{
t\sim \mathrm{Unif}[0,1],\\ \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_d),\\
(\z,\o,\gamma)\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}(\z,\o,\gamma)
}}
\!\!\!\!\Bigl[\bigl\|\mathbf{v}_t^\theta(t\z+(1-t)\epsilon\!\mid\! \o,\gamma)-(\z-\epsilon)\bigr\|^2\Bigr].
\label{eq:cfm_cost}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Discrete-Time Control Barrier Functions}
Let $\mathcal{C}=\{\x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_x}:\; h(\x)\ge 0\}$ denote the safe set for a continuously differentiable $h$.
For the discrete‐time control‐affine system
\begin{equation}
    \x_{k+1}=f(\x_k)+g(\x_k)\u_k, \label{eq:affinesystem}
\end{equation}
A function $h$ is a discrete‐time control barrier function (DCBF) associated with the control system \eqref{eq:affinesystem} if there exists $\gamma\in(0,1]$ such that for all $\x_k$ there exists a control $\u_k$ with
\begin{equation}
    \Delta h(\x_k,\u_k)\;:=\;h(\x_{k+1})-h(\x_k)\;\ge\;-\gamma\,h(\x_k).
    \label{eq:dtcbf}
\end{equation}
Condition \eqref{eq:dtcbf} implies the one‐step decay bound
\begin{equation*}
    h(\x_{k+1})\;\ge\;(1-\gamma)\,h(\x_k)
    \, \Longrightarrow \,
    h(\x_k)\;\ge\;(1-\gamma)^{k}\,h(\x_0),
    \label{eq:exp_decay}
\end{equation*}
\textit{i.e.,} the lower bound on the barrier value decays exponentially with base $(1-\gamma)$, ensuring forward invariance of $\mathcal{C}$ \cite{ames2014cbf,agrawal2017discrete}.

We first define the safety specification derived from a supporting hyperplane to the single obstacle. Let \(\piq:\mathbb R^{n_x} \to \mathbb R^d\) be the projection that maps robot states into the robot position. \(\piq(\xki)=\qki\in\mathbb R^d\) denote the robot position at state \(\mathbf{x}_{k+i \mid k}\) at discrete time step $k$ and horizon $i = 1, ..., H$. For each convex configuration-space obstacle $j$, let $\p_j$ be the point on its boundary closest to the current position $\qk$, with outward normal $\n_j$. The affine barrier function is defined as
\begin{equation}
h_j^{\mathrm{aff}}(\x;\xk) :=
\frac{\n_j^\top(\q-\p)}{\n_j^\top(\q_{k\mid k}-\mathbf{p})}.
\label{eq:aff_barrier}
\end{equation}
Since $\n_j^\top(\qk-\p_j)>0$, the safe set for each horizon $i$ can be defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{aff}}_{i,j}=\{\x \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \mid h_j^{\mathrm{aff}}(\x;\xk)\ge (1-\gamma)^i\},\label{eq:aff_safeset}
\end{equation}
is a convex half-space. Iterating the condition \eqref{eq:dtcbf} from $i=0$ with the normalization $h_j^{\mathrm{aff}}(\xk;\xk)=1$ yields
\begin{equation}
h_j^{\mathrm{aff}}(\xki;\xk) \ge (1-\gamma)^i. \quad i = 1, ..., H.
\label{eq:safetyspecification}
\end{equation}

\noindent \textbf{Nominal Polytope for MPPI safety.} We further define the notion of a \textit{nominal polytope} to generalize the safety definition to multiple obstacles. Let $\mathcal O(\xk)$ index the obstacles observed at the current robot state. We define the nominal ego-centric (\textit{i.e.}, defined as a function of $\xk$) safety function
\begin{equation}
    h^{\mathrm{poly}}(\x;\xk) = \min_{j\in\mathcal O(\xk)} h^{\mathrm{aff}}_j(\x;\x_{k\mid k})
\end{equation}
Accordingly, the safe set for each horizon $i=1,\cdots,H$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{poly}}_i=\{\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \mid h^{\mathrm{poly}}(\x;\xk)\ge (1-\gamma)^i\},\label{eq:poly_safeset}
\end{equation}
Thus, although the complete free space around multiple obstacles is generally nonconvex, $\mathcal C_i^{\mathrm{poly}}$ is a conservative convex inner approximation set obtained by intersecting half-spaces that separates the current robot position from the obstacles. Furthermore, it is constructed as a bounded set by incorporating bounding faces within a sensing range which will be discussed further.

While the proper design of terminal ingredients (\textit{i.e.,} a terminal set and cost) can formally guarantee recursive feasibility in traditional MPC, the non-convex nature of the resulting optimization problem often makes real-time implementation computationally intractable \cite{rawlings2020model}. SBPC methods such as MPPI face similar challenges. Rather than proving recursive feasibility, our approach ensures safety by enforcing DCBF constraints at each time step, which relies on the weaker but more practical condition of pointwise feasibility, formalized in the following assumption.

\begin{assumption}[Pointwise feasibility]
\label{assumption_1}
For the discrete-time control affine system \eqref{eq:affinesystem}, for every replanning state $\xk$, and DCBF parameter $\gamma \in (0,1]$, 
there exists a control sequence\footnote{Assumption~\ref{assumption_1} is the condition for pointwise feasibility of the MPPI-DCBF algorithm at the current state; it ensures that the set of DCBF-certified trajectories for the planner to find is non-empty.}
$\{ \uki \}_{i=0}^{H-1}\in\mathcal U^H$ such that the resulting trajectory $\{\xki\}_{i=1}^{H}$ lies within the sequence of safe sets defined in~\eqref{eq:poly_safeset}, i.e., $\xki \in \mathcal{C}_i^{\mathrm{poly}}$ for all $i \in \{1, \dots, H\}$:
\begin{equation}
h^{\mathrm{poly}}(\xki;\xk)\ge(1-\gamma)^i,
\qquad i=1,\ldots,H.\label{eq:nominalpolytope}
\end{equation}
\end{assumption}

\section{Problem Definition}
% \note{The problem definition section always should occur before the methods section, and after the preliminaries  Perhaps you can combine the LTI and nonlinear system as one case, since the LTI system is also affine in control?}

To generate safety-critical policies, we first formulate our safe planning objective as a constrained optimization problem. To this end, consider $h(\cdot;\xk)$ to be any normalized ego-centric safety function whose nonnegative superlevel set represents modeled safe states at planning step $k$. We hold this function fixed over the prediction horizon and normalize it by $h(\xk;\xk)=1$. This general formulation is \textit{agnostic} to this choice of safety function:
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:sc-mppi}
\begin{align*}
\min_{\{\mathbf{u}_{k:k+H-1\mid k}\}}
& \mathbb{E}\!\left[J(\mathbf x_{k:k+H\mid k},\mathbf u_{k:k+H-1\mid k})
\right], \tag{1a} \label{eq:mppi-dcbf}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
& \eqref{eq:mppi-dyn}, \ \eqref{eq:mppi-set1}, \ \eqref{eq:mppi-set2},\\
& h(\xki;\xk)\ge (1-\gamma)^i. \tag{1e} \label{eq:poly}
\end{align*}
\end{subequations}
Direct optimization using a non-convex $h$ does not generally guarantee recursive feasibility for this problem and often makes real-time implementation computationally intractable~\cite{rawlings2020model}. On the other hand, from the view of set invariance, $\gamma$ modulates the trade-off between \textit{safety and feasibility}~\cite{zeng2021enhancing}. For example, a small $\gamma$ restricts the safety function to higher values, at the cost of reduced feasibility.

\begin{remark} In order to prove $h$ is a valid DCBF, it is necessary to find a pointwise set of control values at any given state that is non-empty, with corresponding $\gamma$. To be compatible with the averaging in MPPI, we assumed that all rollouts \eqref{eq:mppi_rollout} assigned nonzero MPPI weight after rejection based on \eqref{eq:dtcbf}. In practice, we trained the generative policy only on feasible action sets that will be further elaborated upon.
\end{remark}

\section{Method}
Having presented the necessary background on model predictive path integral control, discrete-time barrier functions, and conditional flow matching, we now formulate the proposed framework. At a high level, our approach integrates a flow-matching generative model with a robust safety verifier. To achieve this, MPPI-DCBF first instantiates the generic safety function as a nominal polytope (\textit{i.e.}, $h=h^{\mathrm{poly}}$) as defined in our problem definition (\eqref{eq:mppi-dcbf}). Subsequently, the verifier we introduce preserves the same safety parameter $\gamma$ while admitting a less conservative safety function that depends on the queried action.

\subsection{MPPI-DCBF}
We first propose model predictive path integral control with DCBF-based sample rejection (MPPI-DCBF), which modifies the standard MPPI framework to formally enforce safety. The core of our method is the safety specification in \eqref{eq:poly}, which is designed to guarantee the forward invariance of the sequence of time-varying safe sets $\mathcal{C}_i^{\mathrm{poly}}$ defined in \eqref{eq:poly_safeset}. This is achieved by integrating the DCBF condition directly into the MPPI sampling step. We reject any sampled rollout that violates the condition; consequently, we compute the optimal control sequence \eqref{eq:mppi-update} as a convex combination of only rollouts certified to be safe. Under the pointwise feasibility assumption (Assumption.~\ref{assumption_1}), we can formally analyze the properties of the MPPI-DCBF averaging process.

\begin{proposition} \label{prop: 1}
    For the discrete-time control affine system \eqref{eq:affinesystem}, if all contributing rollouts $(m)$ satisfy $\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+1\mid k} \in \mathcal{C}_1^{\mathrm{poly}}$, then the averaged state $\mathbf{x}^*_{k+1|k}$ under the MPPI-DCBF control is also guaranteed to be in the safe set $\mathcal{C}_1^{\mathrm{poly}}$.
    
    % \begin{proof}
    % Under the control affine dynamics, the state $\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+1\mid k}$ in the next time step is an affine function of the control inputs. Since the admissible inputs are a convex polytope and the control update of MPPI is a convex combination of control perturbations~\eqref{eq:mppi-update}, the resulting state $\mathbf{x}^*_{k+1\mid k}$ is a convex combination of the rollout states:
    % \begin{align}
    % \mathbf{x}^*_{k+1|k} = \sum_m \hat{\omega}_m \mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+1|k}, \nonumber
    % \end{align}
    % where $\hat{\omega}_m \in \mathbb{R}_{> 0}$ and $\sum_m \hat{\omega}_m = 1$ are the importance weights derived from \eqref{eq:mppi-weights} after the rejection of unsafe samples. Moreover, since the safe set $\mathcal{C}_1^{\mathrm{poly}}$ is a convex set, any convex combination of points within it must also lie within the set. Thus, if $\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+1\mid k} \in \mathcal{C}_1^{\mathrm{poly}}$ for all $m$, it follows that $\mathbf{x}^*_{k+1|k} \in \mathcal{C}_1^{\mathrm{poly}}$.
    % \end{proof}
\end{proposition}
\vspace{1mm}

If the dynamics of the system of interest are linear time-invariant (LTI) as
\begin{equation}
    \x_{k+1}=A\x_k + B\u_k,\label{eq:lti}
\end{equation}
then we can make a similar statement about safety along the \textit{entire} trajectory, rather than just a single step in the MPPI averaging process.

\begin{proposition} \label{prop:2}
For the discrete LTI system \eqref{eq:lti} under the same pointwise feasibility assumption, if all contributing rollouts $(m)$ satisfy $\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{k+i\mid k} \in \mathcal{C}_i^{\mathrm{poly}}$ for all steps $i \in \{1, \dots, H\}$, then the entire averaged MPPI trajectory also remains within the sequence of safe sets, \textit{i.e.,} $\mathbf{x}^*_{k+i\mid k} \in \mathcal{C}_i^{\mathrm{poly}}$ for all $i$.
\end{proposition}

\Cref{prop:2} holds to ensure the full-horizon safety of the averaged plan as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:hero}(A). Our approach in leveraging DCBFs in a model predictive fashion draws a parallel to optimization-based methods, such as MPC-CBF~\cite{zeng2021safety}.

\noindent \textbf{Verifier Polytope for Expansion.}
The nominal polytope in \eqref{eq:poly_safeset} is a convex inner approximation imposed on all nonzero-weight MPPI rollouts, thereby enabling the convexity-based averaging argument. In a nonconvex free space, however, a single shared region may exclude collision-free rollouts in other local modes, even if that region is enlarged~\cite{deits2015computing}. Therefore, to expand a generative policy into these unexplored safe modes, it is important to preserve the finite-horizon DCBF conditions while reducing the conservatism of the convex inner approximation relative to the nominal polytope. Given a queried action, the verifier instead evaluates its induced rollout individually at the current receding-horizon step, allowing the separating faces to depend on the queried rollout.

For a queried action \(\hat{\mathbf u}\), let
\(\{\xki\}_{i=0}^{H}\) denotes the resulting trajectory. For each convex configuration-space obstacle \(j\in\mathcal O(\xk)\), represented as \(\mathcal O_j:=\{\q\in\mathbb R^d:\|\q-\oj\|_2\le r_j\},\)
the verifier constructs a separating hyperplane \(\mathbf a_j^\top(\mathbf q-\qk)\le m_j,\)
by solving the maximization problem
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\max_{\aj, m_j}\ & m_j\\
\text{s.t. }\ & \aj^\top(\qki-\qk)\le\big(1-(1-\gamma)^i\big)m_j \; (i=1,\dots,H),\\
& r_j\|\aj\|_2\le \aj^\top(\oj-\qk)-m_j,\\
&\|\aj\|_2\le1,\ 0< m_{\min} \le m_j. \label{eq:socp}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
This is a second-order cone program (SOCP) that can be solved efficiently using 'some' tools. Specifically, the first constraint imposes the safety specification in \eqref{eq:poly} with \(h(\xki;\xk)=1-\aj^\top(\qki-\qk)/m_j\) equal to 1 at the current position and is required to remain at least $(1-\gamma)^i$. The obstacle-separation constraint follows from
\[
\inf_{\q\in\mathcal O_j}\aj^\top(\q-\qk)
=
\aj^\top(\oj-\qk)-r_j\|\aj\|_2
\ge m_j.
\]
The remaining constraints fix the scale of the normal and impose the strictly positive margin $m_{\min}$. 
Let $(\aj^\star,m_j^\star)$ denote a solution of
\eqref{eq:socp} for obstacle $j$. The resulting verifier polytope \footnote{In implementation, we additionally include auxiliary bounding faces around the sensing region, making $\mathcal P$ bounded (e.g., 12 auxiliary faces defined by orange control points are used in Fig.~\ref{fig:validity-verifier-pedestrian})(left).} for a queried action is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal P(\hat{\mathbf u};\o,\gamma):=\bigcap_{j\in\mathcal O(\xk)}
\left\{
\q\in\mathbb R^d:
{\aj^\star}^{\!\top}(\q-\qk)\le m_j^\star
\right\}.
\label{eq:verifierpolytope}
\end{equation}

For brevity, we abbreviate the \(H\) steps of predicted control sequence (or action) as $\{ \hat{\u}_{k+i\mid k}\}_{i=0}^{H-1}$ as \(\hat{\mathbf u}\). Having formulated the optimization problem to verify the existence of a multi-step safety specification, we formally define the \textit{validity} of a queried control policy.
\begin{definition}
Let \(v_\gamma(\hat{\mathbf u};\o) \in \{0, 1\}\) be a indicator function that returns \(1\) if the optimization problem~\eqref{eq:socp} is feasible for action \(\hat{\mathbf u}\) under context \(\o\) and safety parameter \(\gamma\), and \(0\) otherwise. The \textit{valid action set} is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \Omega^\star(\o,\gamma)
    :=
    \left\{
    \hat{\mathbf u}\in\mathcal U^H:
    v_\gamma(\hat{\mathbf u};\o)=1
    \right\},
    \label{eq:verifier-certified-set}
\end{equation}
and action \(\hat{\mathbf u} \in \Omega^\star(\o,\gamma)\) is a \textit{valid} action that guarantees the existence of a verifier polytope~\eqref{eq:verifierpolytope}.
\label{def:verifier}
\end{definition}

With the valid action set established, a natural question would be 'are the nominal safe trajectories generated by the MPPI-DCBF planner guaranteed to be valid under this verifier?' The following proposition formally confirms this relationship.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:nominal-verifier}
Fix $(\o,\gamma)$ and suppose the configuration-space
obstacles are represented as above. Let $\p_j$ and the
outward unit normal $\n_j$ define the nominal face, and let \(d_j:=\n_j^\top(\qk-\p_j)=\|\qk-\oj\|_2-r_j>0.\)
If $m_{\min}\le\min_{j\in\mathcal O(\xk)}d_j$, then every \textit{feasible} action whose resulting trajectory satisfies
\[
h^{\mathrm{poly}}(\xki;\xk)\ge(1-\gamma)^i,
\qquad i=1,\ldots,H,
\]
belongs to $\Omega^\star(\o,\gamma)$.
\end{proposition}
Consequently, every MPPI-DCBF rollout satisfying the nominal polytope condition is a valid action whenever Assumption~\ref{assumption_1} holds. 
In particular, this follows under the LTI conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop:2}, while for a general control-affine system, the queried action must be verified separately.
Furthermore, we can formalize how $\gamma$ explicitly modulates the valid action set. In the following proposition, we show the effect of $\gamma$ during the verification process.
\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:gamma}
For any $\o$ and
$0<\gamma_1\le\gamma_2\le1$,
\begin{equation}
\Omega^\star(\o,\gamma_1)
\subseteq
\Omega^\star(\o,\gamma_2).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\noindent\emph{Proof.}
For every horizon index $i$,
$1-(1-\gamma)^i$ is nondecreasing in $\gamma$.
Thus, any valid action at $\gamma_1$ also satisfies the trajectory constraints at $\gamma_2$, while all remaining constraints in~\eqref{eq:socp} are unchanged. \hfill$\blacksquare$

In the context of offline training, generative policies constrain actions to the distribution of their demonstrations. While Proposition~\ref{prop:nominal-verifier} formally guarantees that the MPPI-DCBF planner allows us to produce rapid valid action priors, relying solely on these demonstrations inherently yields a subset of the distribution of all possible safe actions at given context and required safety level (\textit{e.g.,} $\gamma$). This fundamental limitation strongly motivates us to expand the generative policy distribution beyond the initial demonstrations to discover a broader range of valid actions.

\subsection{Safe Flow Expansion}
We introduce Safe Flow Expansion (Alg.~\ref{alg:sfe}) for the CFM model, which acquires missing safe behavioral modes by first pretraining on the safe action support provided by safety-critical SBPC (\textit{i.e.,} MPPI-DCBF) and iteratively increasing the probability mass on uncertain multi-step safe actions.
% This becomes problematic in OOD scenarios, where the model encounters states unseen during training. To address this distribution shift, recent large-scale imitation learning frameworks attempt to gather massive amounts of human intervention data to fill the coverage gaps \cite{}. However, data collection is inherently unscalable for capturing all possible safety-critical actions. To this end, we instead use the current generative policy to propose candidate action windows, query their validity with the deterministic verifier, and retrain on the resulting verified data~\cite{de2026active}. 

Concretely, for a fixed \((\o,\gamma)\), the policy at expansion round \(n\) induces the \textit{conditional generable set} associated with the probability density lower bound \(\tau\) defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_{\theta_n}^{\tau}(\o,\gamma)
    :=
    \left\{
    \hat{\mathbf u}\in\mathcal U^H:
    p_1^{\theta_n}(\hat{\mathbf u}\mid\o,\gamma)\ge\tau
    \right\},
    \label{eq:conditional-generable-set}
\end{equation}
where $p_1^{\theta_n}(\mathbf u\mid\o,\gamma)$ denotes the conditional policy density at round $n$.

The conditional generable set contains actions to which the policy assigns density at least $\tau$. Here, we recover the pre-trained conditional generable set by setting \(n=0\).

To self-generate samples informative about multi-step safety while staying near the current policy \(\theta_n\), at expansion round \(n\),
we sample\footnote{This is the exact Gibbs solution of the KL-regularized exploration objective.
\(
p_n \in \arg\max_q \{ \mathbb{E}_q[\sigma_n(\phi_s(\hat\u))] - \beta \text{KL}(q \parallel p_1^{\theta_n})\},
\) where $\text{KL}$ denotes Kulback-Liebler divergence.}
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf u}
\sim
\frac{
p_1^{\theta_n}(\hat{\mathbf u}\mid\o,\gamma)
\exp\!\left(
\sigma_n\!\left(\phi_s(\hat{\mathbf u};\o,\gamma)\right)/\beta
\right)
}{
Z_n(\o,\gamma)
},
\label{eq:importancesampling}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
Z_n(\o,\gamma)
=
\int_{\mathcal U^H}
p_1^{\theta_n}(\mathbf u\mid\o,\gamma)
\exp\!\left(
\sigma_n\!\left(\phi_s(\mathbf u;\o,\gamma)\right)/\beta
\right)
d\mathbf u,
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_n$ is the kernel posterior uncertainty returned by \textsc{UpdateUncertainty} in Alg.~\ref{alg:sfe} and $\phi_s$ denotes the penultimate feature of the conditional flow vector field evaluated at the fixed flow-noise level $s$.

\subsection{Safe Flow Expansion analysis}
To characterize when Safe Flow Expansion can extend the conditional generable set~\eqref{eq:conditional-generable-set} toward previously uncovered valid actions, we instantiate the reachability analysis of~\cite{de2026active} in the way that SFE evaluates verifier uncertainty on the noised representation $\phi_s(\hat{\mathbf u};\o,\gamma)$ rather than directly on the raw action space. This representation can assign high similarity to two actions that induce similar policy behaviors even when they are distant in the Euclidean space of $\mathcal U^H$. At the same time, both the conditional policy density $p_1^{\theta}(\hat{\mathbf u}\mid\o,\gamma)$ and the deterministic valid action set~\eqref{eq:verifier-certified-set} are defined in the original action space. We therefore preserve $\mathcal U^H$ as the analysis domain and construct the kernel using the learned feature map $\phi_s$. 

Concretely, for a fixed context $\o$ and a safety parameter $\gamma$, we can construct a frozen feature kernel
\begin{equation}
    k_{\phi}^{\o,\gamma}(\hat{\mathbf u},\hat{\mathbf u}'):= k(\phi_s(\hat{\mathbf u};\o,\gamma),\phi_s(\hat{\mathbf u}';\o,\gamma)).
\end{equation} 
For the following analysis, we freeze $\phi_s$ so that $k_{\phi}^{\o,\gamma}$ remains fixed over the sequence of verified action queries, as required by the kernel confidence sequence that will be followed. 

We next introduce the stochastic Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) logistic feedback model used by
\cite[Thm.~F.1]{de2026active} as a statistical model to estimate the safety of unverified actions. The model is formulated within a noisy flow representation that induces the conditional representation space $\mathcal Z_{\o,\gamma}:=\left\{\phi_s(\hat{\mathbf u};\o,\gamma):\hat{\mathbf u}\in\mathcal U^H\right\}.$
Specifically, for a queried action
$\hat{\mathbf u}$, the bandits model~\cite{pasztor2024bandits} assumes at the fixed $(\o,\gamma)$,
\begin{equation}
Y\mid\hat{\mathbf u}\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}\!\left(\varsigma(g({\phi_s(\hat{\mathbf u};\o,\gamma)}))\right),
\label{eq:logistic-model}
\end{equation}
and define
\begin{equation}
\Omega_*(\o,\gamma) := \{z\in \mathcal Z_{\o,\gamma} : \varsigma(g(z)) \ge h\}, \; h\in(0,1)
\label{eq:valid-repres-set}
\end{equation}
as a \textit{valid conditional representation set}, where $\varsigma$ is a sigmoid function, $g\in\mathcal H_{k_\phi^{\o,\gamma}}$,$\|g\|_{k_\phi^{\o,\gamma}}\le B$. in contrast to the implemented verifier returns the deterministic label $v_\gamma(\hat{\mathbf u};\o)$. Thus, the resulting confidence guarantee

Safe Flow Expansion constructs a monotonically expanding valid subset that the learned generable set is guaranteed to cover. Conditioned on the generable set coverage event $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{cov}}$,
$\tau m_{\gamma,r}(\o)$ is the probability mass lower bound of the conditional generable set, and $m_{\gamma,r}(\o)$ is the volume of $A_{\gamma,r}(\o)$. Therefore, $\tau m_{\gamma,r}(\o)$ lower-bounds the probability that one sample from the expanded CFM model lies in $A_{\gamma,r}(\o)\subseteq\Omega^\star(\o,\gamma)$. Likewise, $\left(1-\tau m_{\gamma,r}(\o)\right)^J$ upper-bounds the probability that none of the $J$ conditionally independent samples lies in this valid action set. Increasing $J$ increases the probability that a batch contains at least one valid action. Exploiting this bound at deployment, however, requires an online verifier mechanism, where this mode-selective generation is a common tool in CFM policy generation~\cite{mizuta2025unified}.

The policy replans at $10$ Hz using the measured position and a finite-difference velocity estimate. \noindent \textbf{CFM model details} The base CFM model uses a vanilla flow-matching velocity network of a single MLP with 3 hidden layers, conditioned on the safety parameter and the context: the relative goal position, the velocity, and local observation at the current time step $h^\text{poly}$ (or history in pedestrian tasks) within a \(2\mathrm{m}\) sensing range encoded as a discretized grid. At every replanning step, our CFM model generates \textit{a single} control sequence of horizon $H=10$ from the expanded model.

\noindent \textbf{Safe Flow Expansion details} At each receding-horizon step, the CFM policy self-generates $K=32$ proposal control sequences. We compute the verifier uncertainty of each, normalize the probability~\eqref{eq:importancesampling}, and resample $B=16$ for verification. This separation lets $K$ control action diversity while bounding verification computation by $B$. Then, from the verified actions among batches, the final optimal action is selected by the prescribed execution cost in the form of a standard MPC cost~\eqref{eq:mppicost}. We estimate uncertainty using an RBF Gaussian Process on $L_2-$normalized 32-dim penultimate representation $\phi_s$ at flow time $s=0.9$ with a task-specific $\beta$ and length scale, calibrated from pretrained embeddings.

We demonstrated that Safe Flow Expansion effectively breaks demonstration bias and discovers novel safe behaviors in OOD obstacle geometries. Since the MPPI-DCBF demonstrations were collected in environments with vertical cylinders, the pretraining data was heavily biased toward planar lateral modes; consequently, the pretrained CFM model carries this bias into unseen OOD scenarios. The two left columns show these details in Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage}(a), where the generated trajectory remains near the demonstration altitude. 

However, as SFE iteratively updates valid OOD actions back into training, the policy discovers novel lateral, over, and under modes absent from the pretraining data across the expansion rounds (second to fourth columns in Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage}(a)). The baseline method frequently collides, where the local guidance leads the robot toward the free space (\textit{e.g.,} over and under the balls) but the pretrained base flow $\mathbf{v}^{\theta_{\text{0}}}$ in~\eqref{eq:cfm-mppi_vectorfield} has a generable set that lacks enough probability mass to recover safety. In contrast, SFE shifts probability mass toward fundamentally new safe modes trained offline.

Safe Flow Expansion (SFE) achieves substantially lower online replanning latency than SBPC and guidance-based CFM (\textit{i.e.,} CFM-MPPI), as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage}(b). We evaluate the deployed configurations on an NVIDIA RTX 4060 Ti, using 512 samples for both MPPI variants, \(\mathrm{NFE}=16\) for both flow policies, and 32 guided proposals for CFM-MPPI. For MPPI, dynamics rollouts dominate runtime; DCBF-based sample rejection adds only \(2.2\,\mathrm{ms}\), increasing replanning latency from \(8.9\) to \(11.1\,\mathrm{ms}\) (\(25\%\)) while enforcing safety.

CFM-MPPI requires \(47\,\mathrm{ms}\) per replan, primarily due to online reward-gradient guidance (\(\sim50\%\)) and subsequent MPPI refinement (\(\sim41\%\)). In contrast, SFE performs a single context encoding and unguided horizon-\(H\) flow generation, with no online dynamics rollouts or verifier queries, requiring only \(0.93\,\mathrm{ms}\). This yields \(12\times\) and \(50\times\) lower latency than MPPI-DCBF and CFM-MPPI, respectively.

While this superior computational efficiency is not surprising for our gradient-free method, computation of our method scales linearly with the planning horizon, \(\mathcal{O}(H)\), while increasing the number of parallel samples \(J\) incurs negligible overhead on our workstation. We therefore exploit this parallelism to study how \(J\) affects task performance and safety.

\section{Conclusion}
Our next step is to scale the framework to systems with extremely large state spaces or where expert demonstrations are prohibitively expensive. In parallel, while our method robustly expands the safe action distribution, tuning the online execution cost $C$ can still shift the distribution away from the prior actions, which remains an important challenge.

While our framework establishes a \textit{probabilistic validity certificate} for the generative model, a single unverified rollout may lack a strict deterministic guarantee. However, as our analysis shows, parallelizing multiple generative actions at each replanning step exponentially reduces the probability of failing to sample a valid action, conditional on the coverage event and online verification. Future work could explore combining our expanded policy with a lightweight run-time safety filter to provide hard guarantees.

Additionally, learning an optimal $\gamma$ schedule directly from data remains an interesting open problem (\textit{e.g.,} rather than a fixed $\gamma$ during deployment). Such dynamic scheduling would allow the system to tightly trade off safety and performance, which can be critical in real-world tasks where the robot must adjust caution to context.
% This paper presented a framework for creating safety-tunable generative control policies for safety-critical tasks. We introduced a novel MPPI-DCBF planner that uses DCBFs to generate a rich dataset of provably safe multi-modal trajectories for linear systems. By training a conditional flow matching model on this data, we produced a real-time policy that can modulate its safety and feasibility via a single parameter at inference time, without the need for online sampling. Our experiments on 2D and 3D systems validate the approach, demonstrating a practical method for deploying safe, adaptable, and high-performance learned controllers.

Sampling-based predictive control (SBPC) algorithms, such as model predictive path integral (MPPI), have become a popular method for robotic motion planning applications due to their computational efficiency on modern computing architectures. However, it is difficult to enforce hard safety constraints for such techniques because they average over samples to realize the final set of control inputs. While individual samples may satisfy a given constraint, the sample average can violate the safety requirement. Similarly, diffusion and flow matching algorithms have become a popular way to generate robot motion trajectories. In this paper, we introduce a novel strategy to learn a conditional generative model that allows for online tuning of a safety parameter. As for sampling-based controllers, it is difficult to enforce hard safety-critical constraints in these methods. To address these limitations, this paper proposes a safety-critical framework for SBPCs and generative trajectory generation, based on the framework of discrete-time control barrier functions (DCBFs). Our DCBF-based method ensures safety during both the sampling and averaging phases of SBPCs, efficiently producing a rich dataset of multi-modal, collision-free behaviors. We then train a conditional flow matching model on these data to learn a generative policy that can be modulated at inference time by a single safety parameter. We demonstrate our approach for cluttered environments for a double integrator and a quadrotor simulation. Our results indicate that the proposed MPPI-DCBF framework consistently achieves superior safety guarantees compared to baseline MPPI methods, together with a context-aware conditional flow matching model, paving the way for real-time, safe SBPC and generative robotic control.

\section{Safe Flow Expansion}
Current generative control policies can imitate safe planners, but their behavior is usually limited to the modes present in the demonstration data. In cluttered navigation, this creates a critical failure mode: a pretrained policy may be locally safe-looking yet globally unimodal, repeatedly choosing the same corridor and failing when that mode is blocked, suboptimal, or out of distribution. Simply sampling more from the policy does not solve this, because uncertainty-driven sampling can over-select high-variance frontier actions that destabilize the learned vector field.

Our experiment isolates this problem in a controlled grid navigation task. We pretrain a conditional flow-matching policy on SafeMPPI-generated control windows, then evaluate whether Safe Flow Expansion can improve closed-loop performance from a fixed start by collecting certified successful rollouts and fine-tuning the policy. Unlike prior diagnostics that focused mainly on coverage or validity metrics, we prioritize deployment-level outcomes: success rate, collision rate, and timeout rate.

Our objective is not to trade off performance and safety. First we would want to address which pre-trained data shows the biggest impact on flow expansion. To this end, we need at least a meaningful amount of rollouts for feature compression, and with Gaussian regression, uncertainty-based importance sampling is conducted at the post-training.  
As an auxiliary study, we investigate the effect of $\text{some parameters}$. 

The central hypothesis is that safe expansion succeeds only when three components are aligned:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item representation: uncertainty must be computed in a meaningful low-dimensional feature space;
    \item data: the pretrained policy must encode enough spatial/safety structure to generalize;
    \item optimization: positive-buffer updates must be curriculum-scheduled so the vector field learns stable safe behavior before frontier modes.
\end{enumerate}
